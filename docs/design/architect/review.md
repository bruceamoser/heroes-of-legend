# The State of the Game

**Date:** 2026-09-27 (cycle 43) · **Book source:** `90f7d11` (chapters at `edbe563`, 84,709 words, 399 pages)
**Previous verdict (2026-09-27, `f7873b1`):** a playable, opinionated, fast fantasy RPG that has decided the to-hit roll is not worth its table time, whose single most valuable next step was to sweep the 90 class abilities the way the 208 cards had been swept.
**What changed in the game since the last review:** the sweep ran and landed (`05:568`, PR #742). The Intellect's *Leverage* now fires: it was re-keyed from an ally's missed attack to an ally's **Weak** hit, and the second dead clause in its own effect ("if the reroll hits") went with it. The 90-entry class-ability corpus was read end to end for the first time in nine tables rather than the eight a heading-level scan finds (the Odd's shelf hides under a `===` header, `05:724`), and it came back clean: one dead trigger, no strictly dominated entry. Its one lead is a family of damage figures printed as a **single number inside a table cell**, which no gate in the toolchain can see; that family is measured and ranked below.

---

## 1. What this game is

**Three lines.** A fantasy adventure game that deletes the to-hit roll: you always connect, and your 3d6 roll decides how badly. No spell slots, no mana, no misses, and the dice never leave the players' hands, including when the monster attacks them. Dying costs a scar and a story rather than a character sheet.

**The fantasy it sells.** Heroic sword and sorcery with the bookkeeping stripped out. "Grab your swords. Stow your spell books. Adventure awaits." Chapter 1 then makes an unusually specific set of promises (`01:74-78`): no d20, attacks always hit, magic always fires, no class restrictions, armour reduces damage rather than making you hard to hit.

**Do the mechanics keep them?**

| Promise (`01:74-78`) | Kept? | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| No d20, 3d6, skill over luck | **Yes** | `06:17`. The bell curve does the work: at +3 a hero's Strong is 37.5% against a flat d20's 25%, and at +5 the Weak band falls to 0.46%, so training beats luck for the whole playable range. |
| Attacks always hit | **Yes, and now with no exception at all** | `06:77`, `13:85`. One roll sets outcome and damage together, with no exception in 25 chapters. A week ago this row carried a caveat: the Intellect's *Leverage* priced an ability on a missed attack. It was re-keyed this cycle (`05:568`, PR #742), so the promise now holds literally as well as in spirit. |
| No slots, no mana, magic always fires | **Yes** | `10:19`, `10:31`. Casting is `3d6 + Knowledge + Arcana` for arcane and `Reason + Religion` for divine. The limit is once per scene (Adept) then once per session (Master), which is spotlight management rather than fuel. |
| No class restrictions | **True literally, priced heavily** | `05:15`. A Home rank 1 is 1 DP and an Opposed rank 3 is 16 (`05:770-773`). A Protector reaching Master in Energy pays 28 DP of ranks plus 8 for the card out of a 44-52 DP career. The promise holds at Novice and fails at the top of another class's tree. That is the design working; the chapter prints the promise without the arithmetic. |
| Armour reduces damage | **Yes** | `16:23`. DR by weight class (1/2/3), one source only, never stacking. The ceiling (`16:27`) is each band's Weak value minus one, which is why the three damage tiers stay distinct above it. |

There is a sixth promise the book makes by behaviour rather than in that list: **the DA never rolls** (`13:105`). It commits all the way, down to "a player rolls for each creature on the DA's behalf" at `13:19`. Very few games at this weight take that position, and it is the most distinctive thing here.

## 2. The moment-to-moment

A round gives one Action, one Movement, one Maneuver, one Reaction (`13:31-42`). The rule that gives a turn its shape is `13:49`: you trade an Action down for an extra Movement or Maneuver, never up. One big thing per turn.

Then the exchange, and here is where the game stops resembling its neighbours. When the knight swings at you, *you* roll (`13:105`, `13:109`). Your Defence roll reads the same tiers as everything else, but the outcome is reversed: rolling high means you take the knight's **Weak** damage. Being attacked is an active beat owned by the player. Enumerated over all 216 rolls at Agility +2 (`scripts/defense-frame-census.py`), a defender takes the attacker's Strong damage 9.3% of the time flat, 2.8% with a Boon and 23.1% with a Bane; adding Parry rank 1 moves those to 4.6%, 1.2% and 13.0%. The whole game's tension sits on that one inversion, and it discriminates across the full range: a Bane is 8x more likely to cost you the big number than a Boon.

**The book's worst month is behind it, and the lesson is worth keeping.** The conversion was printed with the sign carried rather than flipped, so the law sentence at `13:119` contradicted its own six implementations, the cover clause two sentences later, and the Challenge rule beside it (`13:111`). Read as printed, a Pack Tactics wolf pack was 3.3x weaker than its own trait and half cover was 2.5x worse than standing in the open. There was a second half to it: twelve defence-side sites (Hidden in three homes, Prone in two, ch13's cover table, the glossary, Sanctuary's rungs, Beast's Watch) never received the conversion at all, and a layout checker found them only after the sign gate was alive. Both halves are fixed and the class reads clean today. The durable lesson is that in a reversed-roll game the *law sentence* is a site like any other, and a sweep that adds per-site clauses while the law still states the opposite sign makes the book contradictory at every one of them.

Texture, measured rather than felt at: the book's own worked round is three creatures and seven turns, and played out it costs 10 rolls in the round plus 7 initiative rolls, with **0 null turns** and no second roll anywhere. Every combatant had something to do on every turn. A drain of about 17 player dice per round is a real table cost, and it is paid knowingly, because the same rule buys the tension: the party's own dice decide both halves of every exchange.

The interesting decision in an average turn is the Maneuver, and the book is honest that it is the weaker currency. Defend buys a Boon on your Defence roll, Shove is a Challenge contest, weapon maneuvers trade a tier of damage for a condition, and a class ability usually outclasses all of them. The interesting decision is therefore "which of my once-per-scene tools is this round worth", which is a genuine resource choice with no bookkeeping attached, and that is the game's central bet.

Where tension actually lives: **Grit** (`13:345`). At 0 HP you spend a point, reset to full, and roll on the Wound Table. You are not out, you are hurt, and the hurt lingers past the fight (`13:377`). The fight's real pressure is not "will I fall", it is "how much of myself do I spend not to".

## 3. The arc

Creation closes: attributes summing to exactly +3, Background DP of `8 + Knowledge + Fortitude`, 8 class DP at the class's own rates, then the level-1 award. In the caster walkthrough both pools closed to the point (11 of 11, 12 of 12) with no question the book failed to answer.

| Tier | Levels | Band | Grit | Cards | New at the gate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Novice | 1-2 | 4/6/8 | 2 | 2 DP | rank 1, cantrips |
| Adept | 3-6 | 5/8/11 | 3 | 4 DP | rank 2 at level 3, rank 3 at 6 |
| Master | 7+ | 7/10/14 | 4 | 8 DP | rank 3 ceiling, once-per-session abilities |

The felt arc is deliberate and it is not a power curve. **Hits-to-fall is flat: 5.8, 5.9, 5.8 hits to drop a hero at Novice, Adept and Master.** A Master is exactly as droppable as a Novice in hit-count terms. What changes is what one action does and how many times a hero can refuse to fall, and as of this week those refusals are no longer identical. The wound roll now adds one die per wound already carried, capped at three extra (`13:379`), so every Grit spend in the game is strictly worse than the one before it: Novice rolls 3d6 then 4d6, Adept 3 then 4 then 5, Master 3 then 4 then 5 then 6. Measured at the ceiling (`scripts/wound-scaling.py`), a single spend carries a 6.23% chance of Death's Door and a 28.7% chance of a lasting or fatal row, and a Master who spends all four Grit in one fight is 49.3% to be carrying a lasting wound before it ends and 11.4% to have stood at Death's Door.

That is the sharpest edge in the book, and I would keep it. It is capped for the right reason, on the book's own precedent: potence stops at three (`06:63`) and Challenge stops at -6 "so the roll stays a roll" (`06:111`). The felt effect is that the top tier's payoff is not bigger numbers but more refusals to fall, each one riskier. That is a real payoff and it is felt at the table in a way a damage curve is not.

Its price is visible on the sheet. Damage per DP falls 3.00, then 2.00, then 1.29, so a player optimising efficiency is right on the numbers to buy breadth. The intent (a Master buys per-action impact and spotlight, not throughput) is now printed at `10:58`, which closes the last item that asked for a sentence rather than a repair.

There is no dead level anywhere in the career: 23 skills on the same 2/4/8 tiers give a 322 DP sink against a 44-52 DP career, and unspent DP carries over (`18:40`).

## 4. The fantasies

Nine classes, and they are not nine variations of one hero.

**The ones with a real identity.** *Shadow*: Stealth plus Sleight, Vanish, and a maneuver shelf built from being unseen; Sap, Slip Away and Shadow Strike all key off it, and the fantasy ("you never saw me") and the mechanics describe the same character. *Odd*: the wildcard, any two Disciplines from different categories, everything priced Adjacent, and abilities like Wrong Number (flip one die to its opposite face) and Break the Rules (ignore a rule, once per session). It is the only class whose identity is "you decide", and the only one whose abilities are jokes that work. *Unbalanced*: the opposing elemental pair, one ladder at Home and one at Opposed, with backlash that channeling turns into power. It is the only class whose price is printed in its own cost table, the high-risk fantasy arrives on turn one, and this cycle its price table turned out to be the one place in the book where the damage law does not reach (item 2 below). *Intellect*: the best-designed support class in the book, because it makes **information** the resource. Assess reveals a resistance, Deduce reveals a hidden property, Anticipate names a target for +2 DR, and Master Strategist hands the party its opening. A player is given things to know rather than numbers to add.

*Corrected twice, 2026-09-27.* This paragraph first cited *Leverage* as the fourth leg of the Intellect's information game. It could not be: Leverage triggered on a missed attack, and in this game attacks never miss, so the review was carrying the book's own phantom in the same shape the book carries them (cycle 41, `f7873b1`). The card has since been re-keyed and now fires (`05:568`: when an ally hits on a Weak result, that ally may reroll for a damage-tier bump), so the defect is closed. The class's case still does not rest on it, and the reason has changed: Leverage is live but it is a damage-conversion support, not information. The information game is Assess, Deduce, Anticipate and Master Strategist, and it stands on those four.

**The competent middle.** *Arcanist* (Energy plus Fire, the spell-manipulation shelf), *Blade* (Melee plus Stealth, Precision, Swift Blade, and the assassination ladder from Serrated Edge to Assassin's Edge), and *Protector* (Armor plus Shields, and the only class whose fantasy is a number: Interpose, Bulwark, Hold the Line, Unbreakable). All three do exactly what the label says.

**The Shepherd** lands its divine-support fantasy (Gentle Mend, Divine Intervention, and a callout telling you plainly that healing changes every fight), but its second arm is Animal and Plants, so it is really a nature-priest. The name fits. Worth knowing before a new player picks it expecting a cloistered cleric.

**The Leader's entry point is not thin, it is the cheapest shelf in the book.** All four of its Novice options are Maneuvers (`05:612-615`), and a Maneuver is the turn's secondary resource (`21:105`: "One per turn") that can never buy a second Action (`13:51`), so the class attacks with its Action and commands with its Maneuver in the same round. Book-wide the census counts **36 Novice class options: 4 cost a full Action, 11 cost a Maneuver, 21 are triggered riders** (`scripts/class-shelf-cost-census.py`), and the Leader is the only class whose entire Novice shelf is maneuver-priced. What survives is repetition: three of the four are once per scene, which is the span of a Standard fight under the pacing law.

**"Discipline is permission" produces distinct characters, with one caveat.** Every one of the 23 Disciplines now has a card or class ability consuming it, and the price grid (23 rows by 9 classes, four structures) means two heroes of the same class can diverge sharply at rank 2 and 3. At rank 1 they converge: a Novice of any class is buying the same 2 DP cards at different prices. Convergence at rank 1 is not a defect, it is what a shared floor looks like.

**Who I would hand the book to.** A new player gets the **Blade**: attack, and Stealth gives you a second thing to do, with Precision making the arithmetic forgiving. Second choice the **Protector**, because DR 2 and a shield make the first fight survivable and the role is obvious. I would steer a first-timer away from the **Unbalanced**, which asks for two ladders and a backlash table before the core is solid, and whose backlash table is now known to be the book's least settled paragraph of numbers.

## 5. Magic, monsters, and the GM's seat

**Casting.** 15 arcane cantrips, 60 arcane spells, 46 divine. Cantrips are at will and free; a Novice card (4/6/8) is the paid upgrade from a cantrip's 1/3/5 (`10:80-82`). Nothing is ever wasted, and the class fantasy ("fire answers to me") shows up as a distribution rather than a special rule: an Arcanist casting at +3 rolls Strong 37.5% of the time against a Brawn +1 martial's 16.2%, off the same Weak floor. The engine is good. Its weak point was learnability, not power: the procedure named a casting skill it never defined, the Adept cadence was stated two ways, and a 12-card primal tradition sat with no item, no glossary entry and no class. Those are rule-text repairs, and the engine underneath them is sound.

A spell chain reads the way a chain should: pick a Novice card, then an Adept rung that deepens it, then a Master rung that changes the shape of the fight. That is a real progression and it is legible on the page.

**Monsters.** 49 blocks, each a stat line, attributes, one or two attack triples and traits (`20:17`). They have been played, not just tabulated: every block at its own Challenge band, against a mundane and a magical attack, with hero DR at 0 through 3. **47 of 49 are sound.** The two failures were both a block's own text doubling up a reduction or naming a counter that does not exist where the block is used: the Wraith printed Incorporeal *and* DR 3 over the same population, which collapsed its damage spread to a coin flip between 1 and 2 (6.3x damage for a magical weapon), and the Swarm of Rats was immune to single-target attacks with an area effect as its named counter, when no area effect exists in the Novice band where the swarm is budgeted. Both repaired. The pacing gate reads 3.79 / 3.73 / 3.27 rounds against the 3-4 round law, on the whole 49-block corpus, with Grit and DR both in the model.

The bestiary's remaining gap is about priority, not content: **45 of the 49 blocks decide their own turn, but only 8 carry any who-or-when cue, and four of those are only a positioning cue** (`scripts/bestiary-role-census.py`: Wolf, Dire Wolf, Bandit and Hobgoblin say "fight next to an ally"; only the Bugbear `20:180`, the Doppelganger `20:555` and the Cave Troll `20:571` say who to hit). So the reader is not left to invent *what* a creature does, only *who* it does it to and *when* it stops, and the answer to the second is printed in full at `13:307`. `20:32` now says so in the chapter's own voice, and the starter adventure's blocks each carry a `Morale:` clause while the bestiary's carry none. The two populations disagreed and nothing said how; now something does.

**The GM's seat is the strongest chapter in the book.** `19:53` states the encounter budget in the same currency as the pool the party actually has (`HP x (Grit + 1)` plus what Catch Breath returns) and prices the four difficulties as fractions of a day: Easy about half, Standard about three quarters, Hard the whole day, Deadly more than a day. Few published games state drain as a fraction of a real pool. On top of that: field size at 1.5 creatures per hero with the reason printed (`19:57`: party damage grows 1.31x while monster HP stays flat), waves, objectives, "know when to end it", treasure as capability rather than gold (`19:75`: "Capability is earned, granted, or taken; it is never purchased"), NPCs as a want and a fear, a corruption track, rest variants, a faction system with favors, and a four-scene starter adventure that teaches the rules in layers (`19:211`). A new DA can run a good session out of this chapter.

## 6. Cohesion and flavour

The voice is consistent and it is a voice. ch01 argues its own design in a note about why 3d6 rather than asserting it; ch13 is declarative and short ("Roll, sort, go"); ch19 is written to a person ("Your players won't miss what they never saw, but they will remember the session that dragged"). The vocabulary is disciplined: the retired lexicon is gone (no spell slots, no mana, no damage dice, no saving throws), zero em-dashes across 25 chapters, and card flavour sits in the register of its neighbours (458 of 503 rungs carry a clause).

Mechanics and fiction agree about what the game is. The one place they did not has been half repaired and half disclosed: `01:156` now names the substitution that lets a Blade add Agility to a melee weapon (`09:48`'s Precision), so the most-read paragraph in the book no longer asks a new reader to reverse-engineer a class talent. It still stars level-3 characters (`01:144`), which is disclosed in the text rather than fixed, and it is the one teaching example in the book.

**Beside the games it will sit next to.** Against **5e** the exchange is faster and more legible: no to-hit roll, no damage dice, no HP inflation, one Action, and being attacked is a player's roll rather than a passive number. It gives up tactical granularity: no grid-native rules, few conditions, and a Maneuver currency that most characters spend on the same two or three options. Against **Draw Steel** it lacks a drama engine and the hero-versus-minion asymmetry, and its fights run shorter (3.3 to 3.8 rounds against Draw Steel's 5 to 6). What it has that neither does: the DA never rolling, an always-hit flat-damage spine with DR subtraction, and a wound table as the price of surviving rather than a death spiral.

## 7. What is genuinely good

**1. The reversed Defence roll** (`13:119`, `06:122`). The party rolls everything, so no turn is ever passive, and the tension stays with the players instead of the DA's screen. It discriminates across the whole reachable range (9.3% to take a knight's Strong damage flat, 2.8% with a Boon, 23.1% with a Bane, and training a defence skill is worth more than any item), and it is a real design decision rather than a re-skin of an armour class. The proof of how much it carries is that both times the book got its sign wrong, it broke the game at the table rather than just reading oddly.

**2. Grit with the Wound Table** (`13:345`, `13:377-424`). Falling is a decision point and a story, not an exit, and the escalation now makes each refusal to fall cost more than the last: measured against the book's own failure condition (`13:347`, a hero falls when HP reaches 0 with no Grit left), no hero is dropped while holding Grit, the tail opens on one sustained wound in no tier, and the whole tax of a sustained wound is about 0.3 rounds capped at three sources. The D666 table turns that cost into character: Lucky Scrape, Scarred, Karmic Wound, Death's Door, with prose that earns its place ("Someone up there likes you. Or wants you alive for something worse. The DA knows which.").

**3. The encounter frame** (`19:53-59`). The pacing defect was real (2.22 / 1.57 / 1.18 rounds on the old field size) and the repair was the right kind: the frame moved, and no stat block, band or hero number did. It measures 3.79 / 3.73 / 3.27 against the law, on all 49 blocks, with Grit and DR in the model.

## 8. What is weakest, ordered by the harm it does at a table

**1. 41 of 49 creatures leave their target to the DA.** The blocks are sound and they know their own turn; what they do not say is who they attack, which is the decision a DA makes most often and under the most time pressure. The cost is not a broken fight, it is a *duller* one: a creature that hits whoever is nearest, every round, plays as a bag of numbers, and the failure mode (monsters that ignore the caster, or fight to the death when the fiction says otherwise) is the one the pacing frame cannot absorb. The frame sentence installed at `20:32` is the right call for a companion rather than a manual, so the repair is not 49 role lines: it is a priority cue on the handful of blocks where choosing wrong breaks the encounter, an aura or a support caster or an ambusher whose whole design is the target it picks. The book already prints that shape three times (`20:180` Bugbear, `20:555` Doppelganger, `20:571` Cave Troll), so this is a reuse rather than an invention: the blocks that need it are the ones whose printed ability only works against the right target, and the Goblin's `Cowardly` clause is the model for the other axis, already written in the creature's own voice.

**2. The damage law stops at the edge of a table cell, and five sites prove it.** `08:225` says it plainly ("The table is the law. The table is the balance"), and `10:46-48` and `19:561` repeat it: every damaging number is a flat value on the band's row. Five sites print **2**, a figure that is on no live row at any tier (the rows are 4/6/8, 5/8/11, 7/10/14) and is the RETIRED Novice Weak value, which is what these most likely are: the band change's residue, ungraded because every damage gate in the toolchain keys on a slash triple, a healing line, or a `+N damage` verb phrase, and a number alone in a table cell matches none of them. The five: the Backlash Table's face 4 (`05:404`, "an adjacent enemy takes 2 damage"), its face 5 (`05:405`, "You take 2 damage; the target takes +2"), *Toxic Bloom* (`05:711`, a Novice Action dealing 2 and applying Poisoned 2), *Volatile Surge* (`05:712`, allies in the expanded zone take 2), and the fumble table's *Spell Backfire* (`06:200`, "Take 2 force damage yourself"). A new census (`scripts/lone-damage-census.py`, built this cycle; **35 candidates, every one read**) puts the whole family in view, and the harm is concentrated in two of them. `05:405` is unresolvable as written: a player rolling a 5 on the Unbalanced's backlash, mid-turn, has to ask what the target takes "+2" of, and it is also the book's only surviving flat damage rider once the sweep's vocabulary includes `takes +N` rather than only `adds +N damage`. *Toxic Bloom* spends a full Action and its once-per-scene for 2, where the Novice row's own Weak value is 4. The repair needs no new rule: the four earlier band sweeps moved retired figures onto the live row by **position** (retired Novice Weak 2 to live Novice Weak 4), and the same map settles all five.

**3. Adept is the tightest tier in the game and nothing says so.** The pacing gate's own resource model puts a Standard fight at 73% of the pool at Novice, **81% at Adept** and 71% at Master, and the wound measurement puts the only tail in the same place: Adept clears every potence except two sustained defence Banes with all three Grit gone, which is 3.4% of heroes. A tier whose fights eat four fifths of the day's staying power and whose reserve is thinnest against the wound tax is a tier where an unlucky run reads as unfair rather than dangerous. The smallest lever is a sentence, not a number: `19:53`'s rungs are already the frame, and they are the thing the measurement says to move.

**4. The flagship teaching example is still not a level-1 example.** `01:156` is where a new reader learns what a roll looks like, and the pair in it are "seasoned adventurers, several levels into their careers" (`01:144`). The derivation now works, so the example teaches rather than puzzles, but it still shows a hero the reader cannot build in step one.

*Closed this cycle (2026-09-27, cycle 43).* The former item 2, "one class ability can never fire, and the corpus it lives in has never been swept", is closed by PR #742: *Leverage* was re-keyed and all 90 entries of the class shelf were read for the first time. The sweep found exactly one dead trigger, no strictly dominated entry among the ten same-tier same-cost pairs it compared, and the lead now ranked second above. The item survives as a lesson rather than a defect: an unswept corpus was the likeliest place for a dead ability, and it was right by exactly one ability.

## 9. What is missing

- **A second adventure, and a campaign frame.** The book ships one adventure, for level 1, and its closing hook (`19:372`) points at a next adventure that does not exist. Play stops at level 2 in printed material, and a level-7 table has no printed encounter to run. This is the largest hole in the game and it is not a repair.
- **A world chapter.** Thornwell, the Iron Circle, the Sunken Vault and the Argent Circle imply a setting the book never describes.
- **A player-facing tactics primer.** The advice that makes a turn interesting (hold the Maneuver for Defend, get behind cover, trade down deliberately) lives in ch19, which is addressed to the DA.
- **Priority cues on the blocks where the wrong target breaks the encounter.** Small and targeted, per item 1 above.

## 10. The verdict

*Heroes of Legend* is a playable, opinionated, fast fantasy RPG that has decided the to-hit roll is not worth its table time and then built carefully around that decision: every attack lands, every spell fires, the players roll everything including their own defence, and falling costs a scar rather than a character. It is for tables that want heroic sword and sorcery with short rounds and light bookkeeping, and for the DA who would rather narrate than roll. It is playable today, and this week the last paid ability that could never fire was fixed and the corpus it lived in was read end to end for the first time, coming back clean: 90 entries, one defect, none dominated. What is left is smaller and more specific. **The single most valuable thing to do next is to put the book's own law inside its tables: move the five lone damage figures onto the live band, starting with the Unbalanced's backlash table**, because that class's entire fantasy is a risk it prices itself and its price is currently the one place in the book that contradicts the budget, unreadably so on its face 5. After that the ceiling is unchanged and it is not a repair: the game ships one adventure, printed play stops at level 2, and a table that finishes it has nowhere the book tells it to go.
