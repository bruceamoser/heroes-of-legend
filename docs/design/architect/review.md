# The State of the Game

**Written 2026-09-29 (cycle 96 self-review) against `fdc8fa1` on main; manuscript `5b329507ca22`; 24 chapter files plus the index page, 89,861 words of source, 409 pages in the `draft-2026-09-29` build (asset `Heroes-of-Legend.pdf`, sha256 `9f642bad…`, verified by download).**

Previous review: rewritten at cycle 84, corrected in place seven times (cycles 86, 89, 90, 91, 92, 93, 94), most recently at `c5248ed`. Its verdict, in one line: *its middle is finished enough to put in front of a table tomorrow; its top is not.* **That verdict is now superseded. The top is covered.**

**What changed in the game since that review, in the order a player meets it.** Three creatures arrived at the two Challenge rungs that were empty (`20:412`, `20:518`, `20:574`), so the Challenge 7-and-up shelf went from four blocks to seven: a Wild Hunt Rider and a Storm Giant at Challenge 9, an Archdemon at Challenge 11. Each prints a solo figure, so one of them alone is a three-round boss fight rather than a one-round speed bump. Each prints its own tactics — the giant picks the party's tightest knot, the rider marks the weakest hero and calls the hunt onto the same mark, the archdemon eats whatever falls and saves the strongest for last — which is three more blocks on the axis the old review called the bestiary's one real gap (party-facing priority, now 11 of 52 rather than 8 of 49). Nothing else moved: the other 49 blocks are byte-identical to the previous draft, and no rule, card, class or price changed.

---

## 1. What this game is

A fast, legible fantasy adventure game where you roll three dice, the number tells you how hard you hit, and what ends your hero is a scar you chose to take rather than a hit point you failed to roll. It is a game about competent people in a dangerous world: you always connect, your enemies always connect, and the only question at the table is what it costs you.

Chapter 1 makes five promises to a player arriving from another game (`01:73-79`). All five are kept, and two of them are kept better than the promise:

| Promise | Kept? | Where the mechanics answer for it |
|---|---|---|
| No d20, use 3d6, skills over luck | Yes | `06:17`, the core roll. At even odds the three tiers come up 26% / 65% / 9%, so the third of the table that matters is the top; a hero's own +2 turns that into 9% / 65% / 26%. Training, not luck, moves the middle. |
| Attacks always hit | Yes | `06:77`, `13:98`. The roll reads damage; there are no null turns in the game's combat. |
| No slots, no mana, magic always fires | Yes | `10:13`. The roll decides the size of the blast, never whether it happens. |
| No class restrictions, class makes things cheaper | Yes | `08:113` states the one exception (skills have no prerequisites) and the rest is price: a class discounts its Disciplines rather than gating them (`05:779`). |
| Armor reduces damage, it doesn't make you harder to hit | Yes, and this is the strongest version of it | Since the armament collapse, nothing you can hold carries a number: your DR is a card's value capped by your tier, and a shield grants a Boon through a Reaction, one attack a round (`13:109`, `16:112`). |

The pitch a stranger needs is the last row. Every other fantasy game of this size sells a dodge; this one sells the moment after the blow lands.

## 2. The moment-to-moment

A turn is one Action, one Movement, one Maneuver, one Reaction, and unlimited talk (`13:31-47`). Because nothing misses, a turn can never be spent and lost — the floor on a bad roll is a glancing hit, not a whiff. That single decision changes the feel of a round more than any other rule in the book: there is no "my turn did nothing," so the tension moves from *whether* to *how much*, and the table keeps moving.

The interesting decision in an average turn is the Maneuver. That is where the game's real choices live — Defend for the Boon, Aid for an ally's roll, Shove, Grapple, Catch Breath, the weapon maneuvers you earned (`13:54-82`). It is a scarce resource that can never be traded up into a second Action, and the book tells you plainly it is often worth more banked than spent (`13:89`). The census backs the emphasis: of the 36 printed Novice class options, only 4 cost the turn's Action; 11 cost the Maneuver and 21 are triggered riders. The game is designed so that your off-turn options matter as much as your big swing.

The tension is located at 0 HP. Dropping is not death: you spend a Grit to keep fighting, you take a Wound, and the Wound joins a D666 roll you will make the next time you drop (`13:355`, `13:397`). A player who reads that table stops treating Grit as a resource and starts treating it as a countdown they are choosing to shorten. That is the best piece of design in the book and it is doing all the work nobody notices.

One texture note, honestly: for a game this clean, the round is *longer* in its reading than in its playing. The Maneuver list (`13:54`), cover (`13:460`), the conditions table (`13:198`) and the defense-modifier table (`13:132`) are four separate lookups for a turn that resolves in one roll.

## 3. The arc

Creation is fast and produces a person rather than a build (a background, a culture, an ancestry gift, three to five skills, one or two Disciplines, `02:307` works through it with a full character). Ten levels, one every two to three sessions, half a year of weekly play (`18:23`).

The felt curve is the thing to judge, and I measured it rather than reading it. At the book's own encounter frame — five creatures for a party of four, at every tier — an even fight runs **4.00 rounds at Novice, 3.71 at Adept, 3.62 at Master**, and what it costs in Wounds runs the other way: 0.05-0.14 hero-drops a fight at Novice, 0.26-0.66 at Adept, 0.39-1.44 at Master. Tenfold.

**Correction to the previous review, dated.** It said the top of the ladder was "shorter and safer than the bottom." Shorter, yes, by about half a round. *Safer, no.* The top is where the scars are: at Novice a hero is dropped in about one fight in seven to twenty; at Master, between one in three and more than one a fight, and the pool it drains is the same size in relative terms (36% of a hero's between-respite pool at Master against 51% at Novice). The two halves of the game are not the same game, and the difference is the right way round — the bottom teaches you the rules, the top spends what you earned.

What a hero becomes by Master: the damage row moves 4/6/8 to 7/10/14, Grit climbs, DR hits the tier ceiling, three more Discipline ranks and 32 Development Points land on the sheet. On paper that is a bigger number. At the table it is a different posture: at Novice you are one bad round from the floor and you know it; at Master you can stand in the middle of five things and pick which one dies first. The Master tier is a payoff, not just a bigger row.

The one soft spot is the ladder's own clock. The book is candid that a career is ended by the death roll and by nothing else (`18:23`), and it prices its rungs in Wounds (`19:55`): Easy is about a fifth of a Wound per hero per encounter, Standard about a third, Hard one to two, Deadly two or more — and at Novice a Deadly fight spends every Grit the party has and puts the rest of the bill into Dying rather than into Wounds. That means the campaign's *length* is set entirely by which rung the DA chooses to run, and the book says so out loud and tells the table to decide it together. I like this more than I trust it: a novice DA reads "about a third of a Wound" and has no instinct yet for how that compounds. The guidance is there; the instinct is not, and the book cannot give it.

## 4. The fantasies

Nine classes, and eight of them arrive. The Blade, the Protector and the Shadow feel like three answers to the same question, the Arcanist and the Shepherd the two ends of the magic chapter, the Leader the only class whose entire Novice shelf is maneuver-priced — correct for a class whose identity is giving turns away — and the Intellect and the Wilds each carry a real fiction (the scholar who out-thinks the fight, the hunter who owns the terrain).

The Shepherd correction stands from the previous review and I will not relitigate it: its rescue works, and the class is not the weakest on that axis. What is true is that its healing is *gated* rather than printed — the healer's threshold rule and the Catch Breath economy mean a support hero's value is in the timing of a Maneuver, not in a big number, and nothing on the class's own shelf says that in those words.

The class I would hand a player brand new to tabletop: Blade or Protector, in that order — the Blade is a complete character from two decisions, and the Protector's promise ("you stand here, they do not pass") is legible in one sentence. The class I would steer a new player *away* from is the Arcanist, and not because it is weak: it is the most rewarding class in the book and it asks you to build it cold out of a 75-card spell chapter. Arcane caster is a second-character fantasy here, and the book does not warn anyone about that.

Disciplines do produce distinct characters, because discipline is permission: the two characters with the same class and different Discipline picks play nothing alike. The census says a rank-2 floor is met everywhere and no Discipline is unserved, which is the mechanical version of the same claim.

## 5. Magic, monsters, and the GM's seat

**Magic** is the best-shaped subsystem in the book and it has not changed: one cast, no slots, the roll sets the size, chains that go Novice to Adept to Master, and Recurring where a spell should be repeatable. The spell list is 127 cards across two chapters, which is enough to make an Arcanist feel like a project and not enough to drown a new player once they have a lane.

**Monsters.** This is the section that changed, so I re-measured it end to end. 52 blocks; every one stays defeatable at its band and keeps its three damage tiers distinct when played against a real party (52 of 52, no unreachable attacks, no collapsed triples, no immunity without a reachable counter). Every primary attack row sits on its band row (89 rows on-row, none off) and every block sits under its band's DR ceiling. 48 of 52 decide their own turn. The apex shelf — the ten blocks at Challenge 6 and up — clears a lone fight in **2.86 to 4.00 rounds on the printed solo figure**, with zero speed bumps and no exceptions, and 3.6 to 5.0 rounds in its printed group use. The pacing law holds at both ends: three to four rounds, every tier.

Priority is where the bestiary was thin and it is now half-fixed: 11 of 52 blocks name who they hunt or when they stop. The three new blocks are three of those eleven, which is not a coincidence — it is what the previous review asked for and what got built. The remaining 41 are not a defect, and the review should stop calling them one: `20:33` states the position out loud — *the block says what it can do, not what it wants; its target is your call* — and that is the right call for a DA who wants to run a fight rather than follow a script. What was wrong was the *Mix this with a printed plan* gap: some creatures have an intent written into the fiction, and those now say so.

**The GM's seat.** The DA chapter is the strongest chapter in the book. It builds an encounter from a party of four and a party level, prints what each rung costs in staying power and in Wounds, prints the field size, gives the healer's threshold advice with the numbers behind it, and tells you when the fight is over. A first-time DA can read four pages and build a session that lands in the window. That was not true a month ago.

## 6. Cohesion and the games it sits beside

The prose has one voice: plain, second person, warm without being cute, and it keeps its promises about tone — the opening fiction and the rules agree about what the world is (dangerous, wet, and full of people who want things). The book talks to the reader like a person: "say it with me: always hit" (`13:98`) is the whole personality of the rules in six words.

Beside **5e** it is faster and more legible, and it gives up the grid (measured distances, cover as a positional puzzle, the long condition list, the attack roll's whiff). Beside **Draw Steel** it has the same appetite for combat-as-the-main-course and the same refusal to let a turn be wasted, but where Draw Steel carries a drama engine (the Director's malice, the players' resources for pushing) this game puts all the tension in the Wound track and the death roll. That is a real difference in kind, not just in size: **Heroes of Legend** asks the question *how much are you willing to spend to keep going*, and asks it every time someone drops, where Draw Steel asks *whose turn is it to be a hero*. Fights here run 3.6 to 4.0 rounds against Draw Steel's five to six; the game is quicker and more nervous, and it earns that.

What neither of them does and this does: the pooled damage-or-effect attack card. A single roll reads the tier, the tier reads the card's row, and on a Critical the same roll also buys a bonus effect off the Critical table (`13:100-105`) — no second roll, no "I hit, now let me roll damage," no choosing between landing the blow and doing something interesting with it. The book's own line makes the argument better than I can: one big thing per turn, so make it count (`13:51`).

## 7. What is genuinely good

1. **The reversed defense roll** (`06:93-141`, `13:116-131`). The player rolls every die that matters, including the ones aimed at them, and the defender's DR already lives inside the printed numbers. It puts the game's most emotionally loaded moment — being hit — in the player's own hand, and the appendix's explanation of *why* is honest about what it costs (a modifier applied backwards is a Bane, and the chapter prints a table of it, `13:128-157`). No other game at this size does it.
2. **Grit, Wounds, and the death roll** (`13:355-459`). Spending a Grit keeps you in the fight and buys you a chance at a worse death next time. The instrument confirms the ladder does not compound unfairly — no hero is dropped while holding Grit, the Banes that land on the defense roll cost about 0.3 rounds of pool life, and the book's own ceiling of three sources holds (`06:63`) — so it reads as a spiral without being one. That is the hardest thing in game design to do.
3. **Armor as a card, not a number** (`16:13-118`). After the collapse, gear is fiction and the numbers are on the sheet; a shield is a Reaction. It means a character's silhouette is a build decision, not a shopping decision, and it removes the classic mid-session argument about whether a shield's +2 counts on this attack.
4. **The encounter frame and the clock** (`19:53-63`). Two currencies, printed side by side: what a fight costs today and what it costs forever. Nothing in the book better expresses what this game is about.

## 8. What is weakest, ordered by the harm it does at a table

1. **The book has no index, and it tells you to keep it at your elbow** (`01:71`). It says you will dog-ear pages and look things up constantly — true of any game run from a book — and then ships a table of contents, 24 chapters, a glossary of 107 headwords and two reference sheets against a session where the DA needs "Prone" at speed. This is the highest-frequency failure in the book and the only one on this list that costs something at *every* session, including sessions that never touch a rule it is missing.
2. **A level 7 or 8 party meets one monster.** The Challenge 7 rung holds the Stone Giant and the Challenge 8 rung holds the Death Knight; the Lich is the only thing at Challenge 10. Five blocks cover Challenge 6 through 8, which is four levels of play, while twenty-four blocks cover Challenge 1 and 2. Filling 9 and 11 made this visible rather than creating it: a campaign in its second half fields the same stat block three sessions running, and the DA either reskins it or builds a creature — which is the work the bestiary exists to have done.
3. **There is no chase, no pursuit, and no way out.** The book has a real engine for a tense conversation: stakes declared before the first die, the party's face as the only scorer, the NPC setting the Challenge instead of rolling, first to three successes, assists buying Boons (`14:62-99`). It has nothing for a tense chase. Travel is a pace table and one Survival roll (`19:124-152`), and the fiction's own example is "climbing a crumbling wall while something with too many teeth chases you" (`01:95`). At a real table, running away is the most common thing players do that the rules do not adjudicate — and in a game where attacks always hit, being chased is *scarier* than it is anywhere else. That is a design invitation the book has not taken.
4. **Two lookups that mislead rather than fail.** `Recurring` is defined in the magic chapter's prose (`10:60`) and has no glossary headword, so a player who meets it on a healing card cannot look it up. `Piercing` names both a damage type (`13:163`) and a card keyword (`09:283`, `11:169`), so a lookup on the word returns two different rules and no way to tell which one the card meant.

## 9. What is missing

- **Printed play stops where the starter adventure stops.** The sample adventure is four scenes and an ending (`19:213-361`). It is a good on-ramp and it is all there is: no second adventure, no campaign spine through the ten levels, no batch of ready scenes for the Challenge 9 creatures the book now prints. The faction and reputation systems (`19:392-543`) are scaffolding a DA can hang a campaign on, and they are not a campaign. For a book whose pitch is *keep it at the elbow*, the elbow-side content is the thinnest layer.
- **The chase procedure** named in §8.3. This is the one genuinely absent *mechanic*, in a game whose whole promise is that dangerous things cost you something.
- **A ready-reference card for the turn.** §2's honest note: a turn in combat is four lookups (`13:54` for the maneuvers, `13:132` for the defense modifiers, `13:198` for the conditions, `13:460` for cover) plus `16:112` for the shield Reaction, spread across two chapters and the reference sheets. One printed turn-card would remove the book's second-most-common slowdown after the missing index.

## 10. The verdict

This is a complete game. You can hand it to a table that has never played it, build four characters, and run a session tonight that lands in three to four rounds, costs somebody a scar, and leaves everyone wanting the next one. Level 1 through 10 is playable and the promise chapter 1 makes is kept in full — including the two promises ("your armor reduces the damage," "nobody whiffs") that most games make and quietly break. It is for a table that wants fast, nervous fantasy with real reckoning and no bookkeeping; it is not for a table that wants a tactical grid, twenty conditions, or a game where being attacked happens to someone else. It sits comfortably between 5e and Draw Steel and is quicker than both.

Two cycles ago I wrote that its middle was finished and its top was not. The top is covered: its own monsters arrived, they are priced right, they carry their own tactics, and every one of them lands in the window. **It is the book that is unfinished now, not the game.** The single most valuable thing to do next is the index — it is the only work available that makes every session of every campaign better, and without it the book cannot deliver the thing it promises on its fourth page.

**Order of work from this review:** (1) the index; (2) the Challenge 7 and 8 rungs, so the second half of a campaign has its own creatures; (3) a chase procedure; (4) printed play beyond the vault.
