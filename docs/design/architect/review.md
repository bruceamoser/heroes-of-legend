# The State of the Game

**Date:** 2026-09-27 · **Book source:** `aec0df3` (chapters at `6f4769c`, 84,396 words, 399 pages)
**Previous review:** none. This is the first.
**What changed in the game since the last review:** there is nothing to compare against. The book is read
here at the point where all nine subsystems carry an assessment and all seven walkthroughs have been
played, so the record behind the judgements below is eight transcripts and a bestiary that has been
actually fought, not an impression of a manuscript.

---

## 1. What this game is

**Three lines.** A fantasy adventure game that deletes the to-hit roll: you always connect, and your 3d6
roll decides how badly. No spell slots, no mana, no misses, and the dice never leave the players' hands,
including when the monster attacks them. Dying costs a scar and a story rather than a character sheet.

**The fantasy it sells.** Heroic sword and sorcery with the bookkeeping stripped out. "Grab your swords.
Stow your spell books. Adventure awaits." Chapter 1 then makes an unusually specific set of promises
(`01:74-78`): no d20, attacks always hit, magic always fires, no class restrictions, armour reduces damage
rather than making you hard to hit.

**Do the mechanics keep them?**

| Promise (`01:74-78`) | Kept? | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| No d20, 3d6, skill over luck | **Yes** | `06:17`. The bell curve does the work: at +3 a hero's Strong is 37.5% against a flat d20's 25%, and at +5 the Weak band falls to 0.46%, so training beats luck for the whole playable range. |
| Attacks always hit | **Yes** | `06:77`, `13:85`. One roll sets outcome and damage together, with no exception anywhere in 25 chapters. |
| No slots, no mana, magic always fires | **Yes** | `10:19`, `10:31`. Casting is `3d6 + Knowledge + Arcana` for arcane and `Reason + Religion` for divine. The limit is once per scene (Adept) then once per session (Master), which is spotlight management rather than fuel. |
| No class restrictions | **True literally, priced heavily** | `05:13-16` claims a class shapes cost and not limits, and the cost tables make that literal: a Home rank 1 is 1 DP and an Opposed rank 3 is 16 (`05:762-770`). A Protector reaching Master in Energy pays 28 DP of ranks plus 8 for the card, out of a 44-52 DP career. The promise holds at Novice and fails at the top of another class's tree. That is the design working; the chapter just prints the promise without the arithmetic. |
| Armour reduces damage | **Yes** | `16:23`. DR by weight class (1/2/3), one source only, never stacking. The ceiling (`16:25`) is exactly each band's Weak value minus one, which is why the three damage tiers stay distinct above it. |

There is a sixth promise the book makes by behaviour rather than in that list: **the DA never rolls**
(`13:105`). It commits all the way, down to "a player rolls for each creature on the DA's behalf" at
`13:19`. Very few games at this weight take that position, and it is the most distinctive thing here.

## 2. The moment-to-moment

A round gives one Action, one Movement, one Maneuver, one Reaction (`13:29`). The rule that gives a turn
its shape is `13:49`: you trade an Action down for an extra Movement or Maneuver, never up. One big thing
per turn.

Then the exchange, and here is where the game stops resembling its neighbours. When the knight swings at
you, *you* roll (`13:99`, `13:109`). Your Defence roll reads the same tiers as everything else, but the
outcome is reversed: rolling high means you take the knight's **Weak** damage. Being attacked is an active
beat owned by the player, and a good roll converts a dragon's bite into a graze instead of being a passive
number the DA compares against. That inversion is the best idea in the book.

Texture, measured rather than felt at: the book's own worked round is three creatures and seven turns, and
played out it costs 10 rolls in the round plus 7 initiative rolls, with **0 null turns** and no second roll
anywhere. Every combatant had something to do on every turn. A drain of about 17 player dice per round is a
real table cost, and it is paid knowingly, because the same rule buys the tension: the party's own dice
decide both halves of every exchange.

The interesting decision in an average turn is the Maneuver, and the book is honest that it is the weaker
currency. Defend buys a Boon on your Defence roll, Shove is a Challenge contest, weapon maneuvers trade a
tier of damage for a condition, and a class ability usually outclasses all of them. The interesting
decision is therefore "which of my once-per-scene tools is this round worth", which is a genuine resource
choice, but it is a resource choice with no bookkeeping attached, and that is the game's central bet.

Where tension actually lives: **Grit** (`13:340`). At 0 HP you spend a point, reset to full, and roll on the
Wound Table. You are not out, you are hurt, and the hurt lingers past the fight (`13:372`). The fight's
real pressure is not "will I fall", it is "how much of myself do I spend not to".

The honest cost of the inversion: **the book itself got the sign wrong once.** The Defence Roll quick
reference at `13:121` printed cover as a Bane on the Defence roll, while the cover table at `13:433` says
cover means attacks against you have a Bane. On a reversed roll those are opposite outcomes, and measured
over all 216 outcomes against a cultist the mis-print more than doubles the chance the defender takes the
attacker's Strong damage (16.2% to 35.5%). That was found and fixed. It remains the most likely way a
first session goes wrong, because a table that reads the quick reference alone will conclude that taking
cover hurts.

## 3. The arc

Creation is 11 steps: attributes summing to exactly +3, Background DP of `8 + Knowledge + Fortitude`,
8 class DP at the class's own rates, then the level-1 award. The pools close. In the caster walkthrough
both pools closed to the point (11 of 11, 12 of 12) with no question the book failed to answer.

Then the tiers:

| Tier | Levels | Band | Grit | Cards | New at the gate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Novice | 1-2 | 4/6/8 | 2 | 2 DP | rank 1, cantrips |
| Adept | 3-6 | 5/8/11 | 3 | 4 DP | rank 2 at level 3, rank 3 at 6 |
| Master | 7+ | 7/10/14 | 4 | 8 DP | rank 3 ceiling, once-per-session abilities |

The felt arc is deliberate and it is not a power curve. **Hits-to-fall is flat: 5.8, 5.9, 5.8 hits to drop a
hero at Novice, Adept and Master.** A Master is exactly as droppable as a Novice in hit-count terms. What
changes is what one action does and how many times a hero can refuse to fall: Grit goes 2 to 4, the band
climbs about 1.8x, and the Master shelf is built from counterspells, area effects and once-per-session
interventions (Spellstorm, Perfect Recall, Last Stand, One-Off Wonder, Break the Rules). The danger stays
constant and the *means* scale. I would defend that choice; it is why the fights never become
administrative.

Its price is visible on the sheet. Damage per DP falls 3.00, then 2.00, then 1.25, so a player optimising
efficiency is right on the numbers to buy breadth, and the Master shelf reads as a luxury purchase rather
than a bigger hero. The intent (a Master buys per-action impact and spotlight, not throughput) is decided
as a stated default. It needs saying in the book, because the arithmetic currently says the opposite.

There is no dead level anywhere in the career: 23 skills on the same 2/4/8 tiers give a 322 DP sink against
a 44-52 DP career, and DP carries over (`18:70`).

## 4. The fantasies

Nine classes, and they are not nine variations of one hero.

**The ones with a real identity.** *Shadow*: Stealth plus Sleight, Vanish, and a maneuver shelf built from
being unseen; Sap, Slip Away and Shadow Strike all key off it, and the fantasy ("you never saw me") and the
mechanics describe the same character. *Odd*: the wildcard, any two Disciplines from different categories,
everything priced Adjacent, and abilities like Wrong Number (flip one die to its opposite face) and Break
the Rules (ignore a rule, once per session). It is the only class whose identity is "you decide", and the
only one whose abilities are jokes that work. *Unbalanced*: the opposing elemental pair, one ladder at Home
and one at Opposed, with backlash that channeling turns into power. It is the only class whose price is
printed in its own cost table, and the high-risk fantasy arrives on turn one. *Intellect*: the best-designed
support class in the book, because it makes **information** the resource. Assess reveals a resistance,
Deduce reveals a hidden property, Leverage rerolls an ally's miss, Anticipate names a target for +2 DR. A
player is given things to know rather than numbers to add.

**The competent middle.** *Arcanist* (Energy plus Fire, the spell-manipulation shelf), *Blade* (Melee plus
Stealth, Precision, Swift Blade, and the assassination ladder from Serrated Edge to Assassin's Edge), and
*Protector* (Armor plus Shields, and the only class whose fantasy is a number: Interpose, Bulwark, Hold the
Line, Unbreakable). All three do exactly what the label says.

**The Shepherd** lands its divine-support fantasy (Gentle Mend, Divine Intervention, and a callout telling
you plainly that healing changes every fight), but its second arm is Animal and Plants, so it is really a
nature-priest. The name fits. Worth knowing before a new player picks it expecting a cloistered cleric.

**The weakest rank-1 shelf belongs to the Leader.** Its Novice row is Rallying Cry, Read the Room, Battle
Cry and Form Up: three of the four hand out a modifier and end the turn. The class whose fantasy is
decisiveness has the least to decide at level 1; it becomes genuinely good at Adept (Hold Together reduces
damage by 6) and Master (Last Stand, Seize the Moment). The fantasy is right and the entry point is thin.

**"Discipline is permission" produces distinct characters, with one caveat.** Every one of the 23
Disciplines now has a card or class ability consuming it, and the price grid (23 rows by 9 classes, four
structures) means two heroes of the same class can diverge sharply at rank 2 and 3. At rank 1 they converge:
a Novice of any class is buying the same 2 DP cards at different prices. Convergence at rank 1 is not a
defect, it is what a shared floor looks like.

**Who I would hand the book to.** A new player gets the **Blade**: attack, and Stealth gives you a second
thing to do, with Precision making the arithmetic forgiving. Second choice the **Protector**, because DR 2
and a shield make the first fight survivable and the role is obvious. I would steer a first-timer away from
the **Leader** (your turn is administration) and the **Unbalanced** (two ladders plus a backlash table
before the core is solid).

## 5. Magic, monsters, and the GM's seat

**Casting.** 15 arcane cantrips, 60 arcane spells, 46 divine. Cantrips are at will and free; a Novice card
(4/6/8) is the paid upgrade from a cantrip's 1/3/5 (`10:80-82`). Nothing is ever wasted, and the class
fantasy ("fire answers to me") shows up as a distribution rather than a special rule: an Arcanist casting at
+3 rolls Strong 37.5% of the time against a Brawn +1 martial's 16.2%, off the same Weak floor. The engine is
good. Its weak point was learnability, not power: the procedure named a casting skill it never defined, the
Adept cadence was stated two ways, and a 12-card primal tradition sat with no item, no glossary entry and no
class. Those are rule-text repairs, and the engine underneath them is sound.

A spell chain reads the way a chain should: pick a Novice card, then an Adept rung that deepens it, then a
Master rung that changes the shape of the fight. That is a real progression and it is legible on the page.

**Monsters.** 49 blocks, each a stat line, attributes, one or two attack triples and traits (`20:17`). They
have been played, not just tabulated: every block at its own Challenge band, against a mundane and a magical
attack, with hero DR at 0 through 3. **47 of 49 are sound.** The two failures were both a block's own text
doubling up a reduction or naming a counter that does not exist where the block is used: the Wraith printed
Incorporeal *and* DR 3 over the same population, which collapsed its damage spread to a coin flip between 1
and 2 (6.3x damage for a magical weapon), and the Swarm of Rats was immune to single-target attacks with an
area effect as its named counter, when no area effect exists in the Novice band where the swarm is
budgeted. Both repaired. The pacing gate now reads 3.79 / 3.73 / 3.27 rounds against the 3-4 round law, on
the whole 49-block corpus.

The bestiary's real gap is not mechanical: **no block says how to play it.** A DA gets the numbers and
nothing else, so 49 creatures have no role line, no preferred target, no idea what the Green Hag does with
her turn. Compare the trait lines, which do the frame conversion properly (Pack Tactics, Leadership and
Marshal Undead all print "(a Bane on the defender's roll)"). The blocks are sound and dramatically mute.

*Corrected 2026-09-27 (cycle 38).* Measured, "mute" is too strong and lands on the wrong axis: 45 of the
49 blocks decide their own turn, so the reader is not left to invent **what** a creature does, only **who**
it does it to and **when** it stops, and the answer to the second of those is printed in full at `13:307`.
See item 4 under *What is weakest* for the census and the repair.

**The GM's seat is the strongest chapter in the book.** `19:53` states the encounter budget in the same
currency as the pool the party actually has (`HP x (Grit + 1)` plus what Catch Breath returns) and prices
the four difficulties as fractions of a day: Easy about half, Standard about three quarters, Hard the whole
day, Deadly more than a day. Few published games state drain as a fraction of a real pool. On top of that:
field size at 1.5 creatures per hero with the reason printed (`19:57`: party damage grows 1.31x while
monster HP stays flat), waves, objectives, "know when to end it", treasure as capability rather than gold
(`19:73`: "Capability is earned, granted, or taken; it is never purchased"), NPCs as a want and a fear, a
corruption track, rest variants, a faction system with favors, and a four-scene starter adventure that
teaches the rules in layers (`19:211`). A new DA can run a good session out of this chapter.

## 6. Cohesion and flavour

The voice is consistent and it is a voice. ch01 argues its own design in a note about why 3d6 rather than
asserting it; ch13 is declarative and short ("Roll, sort, go"); ch19 is written to a person ("Your players
won't miss what they never saw, but they will remember the session that dragged"). The vocabulary is
disciplined: the retired lexicon is gone (no spell slots, no mana, no damage dice, no saving throws), zero
em-dashes across 25 chapters, and card flavour sits in the register of its neighbours (458 of 503 rungs
carry a clause).

Mechanics and fiction agree about what the game is, with one exception that is a drafting problem rather
than a design one: `01:156` prints "Your shortsword's Standard damage is 2, plus your Agility: 4" in the
example of play. The number is correct (the Basic Melee floor is 2 at Standard, `09:232`, plus Agility via
the Blade's Precision), but the sentence reads as if the total were 6, and the example never names the
substitution that lets a Blade add Agility to a melee weapon. The most-read paragraph in the book asks a
new reader to reverse-engineer a class talent. It also stars level-3 characters (`01:145`), so the flagship
teaching example is not a level-1 example.

**Beside the games it will sit next to.** Against **5e** the exchange is faster and more legible: no to-hit
roll, no damage dice, no HP inflation, one Action, and being attacked is a player's roll rather than a
passive number. It gives up tactical granularity: no grid-native rules, few conditions, and a Maneuver
currency that most characters spend on the same two or three options. Against **Draw Steel** it lacks a
drama engine and the hero-versus-minion asymmetry, and its fights run shorter (3.3 to 3.8 rounds against
Draw Steel's 5 to 6). What it has that neither does: the DA never rolling, an always-hit flat-damage spine
with DR subtraction, and a wound table as the price of surviving rather than a death spiral.

## 7. What is genuinely good

**1. The reversed Defence roll** (`13:99-121`, `06:99`). The party rolls everything, so no turn is ever
passive, and the tension stays with the players instead of the DA's screen. It discriminates across the
whole reachable range (a Master defender still takes a Challenge 3 knight's Strong damage 9.3% of the
time), and it is a real design decision rather than a re-skin of an armour class. The proof of how much it
carries is that one mis-printed sign in the book's own quick reference inverted cover's value.

**2. Grit with the Wound Table** (`13:340`, `13:372-424`). Falling is a decision point and a story, not an
exit. HP stays static so the pools are predictable, and the D666 table turns the cost of surviving into
character: named triples like Lucky Scrape, Scarred, Karmic Wound and Death's Door, with prose that earns
its place ("Someone up there likes you. Or wants you alive for something worse. The DA knows which.").
Full-book consistency behind it: the same table graded across every reachable outcome, no gaps, no
ambiguity.

**3. The encounter frame** (`19:53-59`). The pacing defect was real (2.22 / 1.57 / 1.18 rounds on the old
field size), and the repair was the right kind: the frame moved, no stat block, band or hero number did. It
now measures 3.79 / 3.73 / 3.27 against the law, on all 49 blocks, with Grit and DR both in the model.

## 8. What is weakest, ordered by the harm it does at a table

**1. The inversion is easy to get wrong.** Ranked first because it is the failure most likely to hit a first
session, and because the book already made it once. The cost to a player is concrete: they take the big
number when they believe they took cover, and they conclude the game cheats. The repair is layout discipline
rather than design: anywhere cover, Marked, Pack Tactics or Leadership touches a Defence roll, the converted
sign has to sit beside the number on the same line.

**2. Wounds compound and nobody has measured it.** Every Grit spend is a Wound roll, so a Novice who spends
both Grit in one fight rolls twice, and much of the table is a per-encounter Bane. Two to four stacked Banes
across a party is the difference between a 3-4 round fight and a rout. I do not know whether the Wound Table
is a spiral or the right dose of grit, and it is the one live unknown that could change the pacing verdict.
This is the single most valuable thing left to do.

**3. A Master's payoff is thinner than the fiction implies.** Damage per DP falls 3.00 to 2.00 to 1.25
(`10:46-48`), so the top of the shelf is spotlight effects while hits-to-fall stays flat at 5.8. A player who
reaches level 7 looking for a bigger hero finds a more versatile one. Bearable, stated as intent, and it
needs the sentence that says so.

**4. The bestiary is mute.** 49 mechanically sound entries with no role or tactics clause. A new DA has to
invent how every creature behaves, and the chapter that teaches encounter building is a different chapter.

*Corrected 2026-09-27 (cycle 38), by census rather than by re-reading.* The blocks are not mute about
what a creature **does**: 45 of the 49 decide their own turn (a Multiattack, a Recharge ability, a
condition on a hit, a second attack line, a casting line, a positioning or support clause, a reaction),
and the four that do not (Swarm of Rats, Guard, Zombie, Fire Elemental) are simple by content, with an
unambiguous turn. What is nearly absent is priority: 8 of 49 carry any who-or-when cue and four of those
are only "fight next to an ally". So the DA's invention is smaller than this item claims, and it lives
on two axes, not on 49 blank entries. The second axis was already printed in full at `13:307` (Morale),
and the starter adventure's blocks each carry a `Morale:` clause (`19:312`, `19:351`) while the bestiary's
carry none, so the two stat-line populations disagreed and nothing said how. Repaired in ch20 with one
paragraph naming both axes and a label on the Morale section: PR #734. The item stands, twice smaller
than written.

**5. The Leader's Novice turn is administration.** Three of four rank-1 options grant a modifier and pass.

**6. The example of play needs a second pass.** `01:156` teaches a number the reader cannot reproduce
without finding a class talent, and stars level-3 heroes in the chapter aimed at people who have never
rolled a d20.

## 9. What is missing

- **The Wound Table measured against the pacing law.** Nothing else can settle whether the 3-4 round frame
  holds once Grit starts costing.
- **Creature role lines.** One clause per block: what it does with its turn.
- **A player-facing tactics primer.** The advice that makes a turn interesting (hold the Maneuver for
  Defend, get behind cover, trade down deliberately) lives in ch19, which is addressed to the DA.
- **A campaign frame and a second adventure.** The book ships one adventure, for level 1, and its closing
  hook (`19:375`) points at a next adventure that does not exist. Play stops at level 2 in printed material.
  A level-7 table has no printed encounter to run.
- **A world chapter.** Thornwell, the Iron Circle, the Sunken Vault and the Argent Circle imply a setting
  the book never describes.

## 10. The verdict

*Heroes of Legend* is a playable, opinionated, fast fantasy RPG that has decided the to-hit roll is not
worth its table time and then built carefully around that decision: every attack lands, every spell fires,
the players roll everything including their own defence, and falling costs a scar rather than a character.
It is for tables that want heroic sword and sorcery with short rounds and light bookkeeping, and for the DA
who would rather narrate than roll. It is playable today: a new player can build a hero and resolve a scene
from the book alone, across four transcripts that needed no invention, and the damage, DR and pacing laws
are measured against the live corpus rather than asserted. It is not yet a campaign. It holds one
adventure, its GM tools are stronger than its monster entries, its bestiary is sound but silent, and its
Master tier promises a bigger hero than it delivers. **The single most valuable thing to do next is to
measure the Wound Table against the pacing law**, because if Grit's cost compounds faster than the pools
absorb it, the 3-4 round frame is holding a spiral rather than a fight, and every other judgement in this
review sits on top of that frame.
