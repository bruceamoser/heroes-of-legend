# The State of the Game

**Date:** 2026-09-27 (cycle 54) · **Book source:** `f64c94f` (chapters at `f64c94f`, 86,405 words, 403 pages)
**Previous verdict (2026-09-27, `29de35e`, cycle 45):** a playable, opinionated, fast fantasy RPG that has decided the to-hit roll is not worth its table time, whose single most valuable next step was to put the book's own law inside its tables and move the five lone damage figures onto the live band.
**What changed in the game since that review:** the Wound Table acquired a price and an ending, and it is now the sharpest clock in the book. Nothing removes a Wound any more (#774, PR #775): a long rest, a Medicine check and magic all return HP, and none of them takes a mark off the sheet, so Wounds became the only track in the game that rises and never falls. The arc then got the ending it was owed (PR #783, `13:454`): four Wounds is where the roll stops escalating, so a fifth would change no number, and it ends the hero instead: they finish the fight they are in, then retire. **Corrected 2026-09-28:** that ending was wrong and is gone. Bruce ruled wounds uncapped (issue #837, landed PR #840, ledger row 244): the plateau is a milestone rather than an exit, and nothing retires a hero but the death roll. Healing was rebuilt around the same judgement (#756, `08:240`): a healing spell carries no row on any table and takes the target to a *threshold* of their maximum, so the ladder is not in the numbers at all, and a healer's tiers buy reach, targets and riders rather than bigger amounts. The Life tree got a spine to match (PR #780): *Staunch* stops a bleed on the bleeding's own axis and prices no HP at all, *Set Bone* mends the bone for an Action. And the players finally got the advice the book had been giving only the DA (PR #784, `13:85`). One survivor of the permanence sweep was repaired while writing this (PR #786, `19:486`).

---

## 1. What this game is

**Three lines.** A fantasy adventure game that deletes the to-hit roll: you always connect, and your 3d6 roll decides how badly. No spell slots, no mana, no misses, and the dice never leave the players' hands, including when the monster attacks them. Surviving costs a scar, and a scar is permanent, so the sheet fills up with a life.

**The fantasy it sells.** Heroic sword and sorcery with the bookkeeping stripped out. Chapter 1 makes an unusually specific set of promises (`01:74-78`): no d20, attacks always hit, magic always fires, no class restrictions, armour reduces damage rather than making you hard to hit.

**Do the mechanics keep them?**

| Promise (`01:74-78`) | Kept? | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| No d20, 3d6, skill over luck | **Yes** | `06:45`. At +3 a hero's Strong is 37.5% against a flat d20's 25%, and at +5 the Weak band falls to 0.46%, so training beats luck across the whole playable range. |
| Attacks always hit | **Yes, with no exception at all** | `13:97`, `13:85`. One roll sets outcome and damage together, with no exception in 25 chapters. |
| No slots, no mana, magic always fires | **Yes** | `10:31`. Casting is `3d6 + Knowledge + Arcana` or `Reason + Religion`. The Adept and Master limits are spotlight management (`10:106-108`), not fuel. |
| No class restrictions | **True literally, priced heavily** | `05:15`. A Home rank 1 is 1 DP and an Opposed rank 3 is 16. A Protector reaching Master in Energy pays 28 DP of ranks plus 8 for the card out of a 44-52 DP career. The promise holds at Novice and fails at the top of another class's tree. That is the design working; the chapter prints the promise without the arithmetic. |
| Armour reduces damage | **Yes** | `16:23`. DR by weight class (1/2/3), one source only, never stacking, ceiling at each band's Weak value minus one (`16:27`). |

There is a sixth promise the book makes by behaviour rather than in that list: **the DA never rolls** (`13:117`). It commits all the way, down to "a player rolls for each creature on the DA's behalf" (`13:19`). Very few games at this weight take that position, and it is the most distinctive thing here.

## 2. The moment-to-moment

A round gives one Action, one Movement, one Maneuver, one Reaction (`13:31-42`). The rule that gives a turn its shape is `13:51`: you trade an Action down for an extra Movement or Maneuver, never up. One big thing per turn.

Then the exchange, and here is where the game stops resembling its neighbours. When the knight swings at you, *you* roll (`13:115`, `13:117`). Your Defence roll reads the same tiers as everything else, but the outcome is reversed: rolling high means you take the knight's **Weak** damage. Being attacked is an active beat owned by the player. Enumerated over all 216 rolls at Agility +2 (`scripts/defense-frame-census.py`), a defender takes the attacker's Strong damage 9.3% of the time flat, 2.8% with a Boon and 23.1% with a Bane; adding Parry rank 1 moves those to 4.6%, 1.2% and 13.0%. The whole game's tension sits on that one inversion, and it discriminates across the full reachable range.

**The book's worst month is behind it, and the lesson is worth keeping.** The defence-frame conversion was first printed with the sign carried rather than flipped, so the law sentence that governs the roll (`06:140`) contradicted its own six implementations, the cover clause two sentences later, and the Challenge rule beside it. Read as printed, a Pack Tactics wolf pack was 3.3x weaker than its own trait and half cover was 2.5x worse than standing in the open. Twelve defence-side sites never received the conversion at all and were found only after the sign gate was alive. Both halves are fixed. The durable lesson is that in a reversed-roll game the *law sentence* is a site like any other.

**The players now hold the tactics.** `13:85` *Playing Your Turn Well*, landed this week, is six bullets: bank the Maneuver for Defend rather than spend it on movement you did not need, cover is free and converts to a Boon on your own roll, an unspent Reaction is a turn you paid for and did not use, count actions rather than hit points and focus fire, magic always fires so the caster stands behind the line, and *Catch Breath* is paid for in attacks not made. Every bullet is traceable to a printed rule, and the section is placed where a reader meets it before their first attack. This was the previous review's first-ranked missing item and it is the best addition to the book this week: the advice that makes a turn interesting had been addressed to the wrong reader for the whole life of the project.

**Texture, measured.** The book's own worked round is three creatures and seven turns, and played out it costs 10 rolls in the round plus 7 initiative rolls, with **0 null turns** and no second roll anywhere (W-003). Every combatant had something to do on every turn. About 17 player dice per round is a real table cost, knowingly paid, because the same rule buys the tension: the party's own dice decide both halves of every exchange.

The interesting decision in an average turn is the Maneuver, and the book is honest that it is the weaker currency. Defend buys a Boon on your Defence roll, Shove is a Challenge contest, weapon maneuvers trade a tier of damage for a condition, and a class ability usually outclasses all of them. The interesting decision is therefore "which of my once-per-scene tools is this round worth", which is a genuine resource choice with no bookkeeping attached, and that is the game's central bet.

**Where tension actually lives: Grit, and it just got more expensive.** At 0 HP you take a Wound and choose (`13:355`): spend a Grit and keep fighting, or stay down and let the Dying roll decide. You are not out, you are hurt, and as of this month the hurt is permanent (`13:450`): nothing gives a Wound back, not a rest, not Medicine, not magic. So the fight's real pressure is no longer "how much of myself do I spend not to fall" but "how much of my *career* do I spend not to fall", which is a heavier question and a better one. It is also the question section 8 now ranks first.

## 3. The arc

Creation closes: attributes summing to exactly +3, Background DP of `8 + Knowledge + Fortitude`, 8 class DP at the class's own rates, then the level-1 award. In the caster walkthrough both pools closed to the point (11 of 11, 12 of 12) with no question the book failed to answer.

| Tier | Levels | Band | Grit | Cards | New at the gate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Novice | 1-2 | 4/6/8 | 2 | 2 DP | rank 1, cantrips |
| Adept | 3-6 | 5/8/11 | 3 | 4 DP | rank 2 at level 3, rank 3 at 6 |
| Master | 7+ | 7/10/14 | 4 | 8 DP | rank 3 ceiling, once-per-session abilities |

The felt arc is deliberate and it is not a power curve. **Hits-to-fall is flat: 5.8 / 5.9 / 5.8 hits to drop a hero at Novice, Adept and Master** (`scripts/damage-curve.py`; the same gate reads about 7.2 / 6.8 / 6.6 on a trained defender at HP 11, an erratum already on the assessment's file). A Master is exactly as droppable as a Novice in hit-count terms. What changes is what one action does and how many times a hero can refuse to fall, and those refusals are no longer identical: the wound roll adds a die per Wound already carried, capped at three extra (`13:387`), so every Grit spend is strictly worse than the one before it. Measured at the ceiling (`scripts/wound-scaling.py`), a single spend carries a **6.23%** chance of Death's Door and a **28.7%** chance of a lasting or fatal row, against 13.48% and 48.5% with no cap at all, which is why three is the right place to stop.

That is the sharpest edge in the book and I would keep it. It is capped on the book's own precedent: potence stops at three (`06:63`) and Challenge stops at -6 "so the roll stays a roll" (`06:111`). The top tier's payoff is not bigger numbers but more refusals to fall, each one riskier, and that is felt at a table in a way a damage curve is not.

**The arc now has an ending, which it did not have a week ago, and the ending is the best writing in the book.** `13:454`: four Wounds is where the track runs out, a fifth would change no number, so it ends the hero instead: they finish the fight they are in, and then retire, "someone the party visits, someone who sends word from a far town, someone who rides out one last time when it matters. Retiring a hero is not a loss. It is the best object this game makes, a sheet that is a list of everything they survived, carried to the end on their own terms." A game whose character sheet is a biography rather than a build is a real position, and this is the paragraph that takes it. Its price is section 8 item 1, and the price is not yet printed anywhere.

**The Life tree grew in a new direction rather than upward.** Healing thresholds (`08:238-251`) mean a Life card never climbs a row, so a Novice heal is worth an Action at Master too, and the tiers buy reach, targets and riders. In a game whose damage ladder is flat by design this is coherent, and it deletes the per-tier healing rows that used to make an Adept heal strictly a Novice heal with a bigger number. It has two costs. The first is that a healer's felt power does not rise with their rank: what changes is where they stand and how many they touch, and the party-wide version is deliberately gated behind Master and once per session (*Tide of Life*, moved from Adept this week), so the moment is a moment and not a default. The second is that every printed healing *item* is now far below a spell: a relic restores 2 HP (`17:137`) where a Novice Life spell returns a hero to full for an Action. Since items ladder by rarity and magic items are found rather than bought (`19:73`), that is the price the ruling named for itself, but a DA handing out a healing relic should know it is a consolation prize beside a party healer.

**There is no dead level anywhere in the career.** The skill list uses the same 2/4/8 tiers as everything else and is far larger than one career can buy, so breadth is always purchasable, and unspent DP carries over (`18:40`). Damage per DP *falls* as the tier climbs (3.00, then 2.00, then 1.29), which is printed at `10:58`: a Master buys per-action impact and spotlight, not throughput. A player optimising efficiency is right on the numbers to buy breadth, and the book now says so out loud instead of leaving it as a trap.

## 4. The fantasies

Nine classes, and they are not nine variations of one hero.

**The ones with a real identity.** *Shadow*: Stealth plus Sleight, Vanish, and a maneuver shelf built from being unseen; Sap, Slip Away and Shadow Strike all key off it, so the fantasy ("you never saw me") and the mechanics describe the same character. *Odd*: the wildcard, any two Disciplines from different categories, everything priced Adjacent, with abilities like Wrong Number (flip one die to its opposite face) and Break the Rules (ignore a rule, once per session). It is the only class whose identity is "you decide", and the only one whose abilities are jokes that work. *Unbalanced*: the opposing elemental pair, one ladder at Home and one at Opposed, with backlash that channeling turns into power. It is the only class whose price is printed in its own cost table, and the high-risk fantasy arrives on turn one. *Intellect*: the best-designed support class in the book, because it makes **information** the resource. Assess reveals a resistance, Deduce reveals a hidden property, Anticipate names a target for DR, and Master Strategist hands the party its opening. A player is given things to know rather than numbers to add.

*Corrected twice, 2026-09-27.* This paragraph first cited *Leverage* as the fourth leg of the Intellect's information game. It could not be: Leverage triggered on a missed attack, and in this game attacks never miss, so the review was carrying the book's own phantom in the same shape the book carries them. The card has since been re-keyed and now fires (`05:568`), but the class's case still does not rest on it: Leverage is a damage-conversion support, not information, and the information game stands on Assess, Deduce, Anticipate and Master Strategist.

**The competent middle.** *Arcanist* (Energy plus Fire, the spell-manipulation shelf), *Blade* (Melee plus Stealth, Precision, Swift Blade, the assassination ladder from Serrated Edge to Assassin's Edge), and *Protector* (Armor plus Shields, and the only class whose fantasy is a number: Interpose, Bulwark, Hold the Line, Unbreakable). All three do exactly what the label says.

**The Shepherd** lands its divine-support fantasy (Gentle Mend, Divine Intervention, and a callout telling you plainly that healing changes every fight), and this week the class also became the one that answers the Wound Table: *Staunch* stops a bleed where a Maneuver belongs, *Set Bone* mends a broken bone for an Action. Its second arm is Animal and Plants, so it is really a nature-priest. The name fits; worth knowing before a new player picks it expecting a cloistered cleric.

**The Leader's entry point is not thin, it is the cheapest shelf in the book.** All four of its Novice options are Maneuvers, the only class whose whole Novice shelf is (`scripts/class-shelf-cost-census.py`), and a Maneuver is the turn's secondary resource (`21:105`) that can never buy a second Action (`13:51`), so the class attacks with its Action and commands with its Maneuver in the same round. Book-wide the census counts **36 Novice class options: 4 cost a full Action, 11 cost a Maneuver, 21 are triggered riders** (`scripts/class-shelf-cost-census.py`), and the Leader is the only class whose entire Novice shelf is maneuver-priced. What survives is repetition: three of the four are once per scene, which is the span of a Standard fight.

**"Discipline is permission" produces distinct characters, with one caveat.** Every one of the 23 Disciplines now has a card or class ability consuming it, and the price grid (23 rows by 9 classes, four structures) means two heroes of the same class can diverge sharply at rank 2 and 3. At rank 1 they converge: a Novice of any class is buying the same 2 DP cards at different prices. Convergence at rank 1 is not a defect, it is what a shared floor looks like.

**Who I would hand the book to.** A new player gets the **Blade**: attack, and Stealth gives you a second thing to do, with Precision making the arithmetic forgiving. Second choice the **Protector**, because DR 2 and a shield make the first fight survivable and the role is obvious. I would steer a first-timer away from the **Unbalanced**, which asks for two ladders and a backlash table before the core is solid.

## 5. Magic, monsters, and the GM's seat

**Casting.** 15 arcane cantrips, 60 arcane spells, 46 divine. Cantrips are at will and free; a Novice card is the paid upgrade from a cantrip's 1/3/5, which sits outside the damage budget entirely (`10:80`, `10:82`). Nothing is ever wasted, and the class fantasy shows up as a distribution rather than a special rule: an Arcanist casting at +3 rolls Strong 37.5% of the time against a Brawn +1 martial's 16.2%, off the same Weak floor. The engine is good, and this week it got more legible: the healing law now lives in one place (`08:238`) with its exclusions written down, so a card author can see that a potion keeps its printed amount while a spell does not.

A spell chain reads the way a chain should: pick a Novice card, then an Adept rung that deepens it, then a Master rung that changes the shape of the fight. The healing chain is the exception and reads *sideways* by design: the same thresholds at Touch, then at 30 ft, then across the party. That is a real progression for a support caster, but it is a progression in positioning, not in power, and a player who buys up the Life tree expecting the damage ladder's shape will be surprised. The book says so (`08:249`); it is the one place in the book where the tier ladder is not a ladder.

**Monsters.** 49 blocks, each a stat line, attributes, one or two attack triples and traits (`20:24`). They have been played, not just tabulated: every block at its own Challenge band, against a mundane and a magical attack, with hero DR at 0 through 3. **47 of 49 are sound.** The two failures were both a block's own text doubling up a reduction or naming a counter that does not exist where the block is used (the Wraith's Incorporeal *and* DR 3 over the same population; the Swarm of Rats immune to single-target attacks with an area effect as its counter, when no area effect exists in the Novice band). Both repaired. The pacing gate reads 3.79 / 3.73 / 3.27 rounds against the 3-4 round law, on the whole corpus, with Grit and DR both in the model.

The bestiary's remaining gap is about priority, not content: **45 of the 49 blocks decide their own turn, but only 8 carry any who-or-when cue, and 41 carry no party-facing cue at all** (`scripts/bestiary-role-census.py`, re-run for this review). The blocks that cannot afford to be played as "hit the nearest" number five, not 41, and all five now carry their cue (PR #751: the Knight, the Archmage, the Orc Warchief, the Green Hag and the Death Knight). For the other 36 the frame is printed and says so in the chapter's own voice (`20:32`), with the who-stops-fighting axis in full at `13:317`. That is a companion's answer rather than a manual's, and I would leave it.

**The GM's seat is the strongest chapter in the book, and this cycle I found the one thing wrong with it.** `19:53` states the encounter budget in the same currency as the pool the party actually has (`HP x (Grit + 1)` plus what Catch Breath returns) and prices the four difficulties as fractions of a day: Easy about half, Standard about three quarters, Hard the whole day, Deadly more than a day. Few published games state drain as a fraction of a real pool. On top of that: field size at 1.5 creatures per hero with the reason printed (`19:57`), waves, objectives, "know when to end it", treasure as capability rather than gold (`19:81`), NPCs as a want and a fear, a corruption track, rest variants, a faction system with favors, and a four-scene starter adventure that teaches the rules in layers (`19:211`). A new DA can run a good session out of this chapter.

But the ladder is priced in **staying power**, and staying power comes back. The currency that does not come back is the Wound, and no rung on the ladder says what it costs in that. That is section 8 item 1, and it is the repair this chapter needs.

## 6. Cohesion and flavour

The voice is consistent and it is a voice. ch01 argues its own design in a note about why 3d6 rather than asserting it; ch13 is declarative and short ("Roll, sort, go") and its new tactics section is written in the same clipped register; ch19 is written to a person ("Your players won't miss what they never saw, but they will remember the session that dragged"). The vocabulary is disciplined: the retired lexicon is gone (no spell slots, no mana, no damage dice, no saving throws), zero em-dashes across 25 chapters, and card flavour sits in the register of its neighbours.

Mechanics and fiction agree about what the game is, and this month they agree about something darker and better: a hero accumulates. The one place they still do not is `01:156`, the book's only teaching example, which stars level-3 characters (`01:144`) and discloses it rather than fixing it. It is the one teaching example in the book and it is still not a level-1 one.

**Beside the games it will sit next to.** Against **5e** the exchange is faster and more legible: no to-hit roll, no damage dice, no HP inflation, one Action, and being attacked is a player's roll rather than a passive number. It gives up tactical granularity: no grid-native rules, few conditions, and a Maneuver currency most characters spend on the same two or three options. Against **Draw Steel** it lacks a drama engine and the hero-versus-minion asymmetry, and its fights run shorter (3.3 to 3.8 rounds against Draw Steel's 5 to 6). What it has that neither does: the DA never rolling, an always-hit flat-damage spine with DR subtraction, and a wound table as the price of surviving rather than a death spiral. Against both it now has something no mainstream game has: a stated end to a character that is not death. 5e retires heroes by campaign ending; this book retires them by biography, and prints the paragraph that says so.

## 7. What is genuinely good

**1. The reversed Defence roll** (`13:119`, `06:71`). The party rolls everything, so no turn is ever passive, and the tension stays with the players instead of the DA's screen. It discriminates across the whole reachable range, and it is a real design decision rather than a re-skin of an armour class. The proof of how much it carries is that both times the book got its sign wrong, it broke the game at the table rather than just reading oddly.

**2. Grit with the Wound Table, which is now a life** (`13:355`, `13:400-446`, `13:454`). Falling is a decision point and a story, not an exit. Measured against the book's own failure condition (a drop taken with Grit still in hand is the pool failing, `scripts/wound-spiral.py`), no hero is dropped while holding Grit, no tier's tail opens on one sustained wound, and the whole tax of a sustained wound is about 0.3 rounds capped at three sources (`scripts/wound-spiral.py`, PASS; the Adept tail is 4.3% of heroes and only once every Grit is gone). The D666 table turns that cost into character: Lucky Scrape, Scarred, Karmic Wound, Death's Door, with prose that earns its place ("Someone up there likes you. Or wants you alive for something worse. The DA knows which."). And as of this week it ends: four Wounds and the hero retires, which is the most interesting thing this game does that its neighbours cannot do at all.

**3. The healing law's shape** (`08:238-251`). "The healer's Action answers the moment, not the arithmetic" is a sentence that closes a question most games never ask, and it is enforced by the arithmetic rather than the flavour: threshold healing moves HP, not Wounds, and the measurement says the party's cost is the Action either way. It also cleaned up after itself: the tier rows it replaced were the last place in the book where a higher tier printed a bigger number for the same effect.

**4. The encounter frame** (`19:53-59`). The pacing defect was real (2.22 / 1.57 / 1.18 rounds on the old field size) and the repair was the right kind: the frame moved, and no stat block, band or hero number did. It measures 3.79 / 3.73 / 3.27 against the law, on all 49 blocks, with Grit and DR in the model. Its currency is wrong for one question (section 8 item 1) and right for everything else.

## 8. What is weakest, ordered by the harm it does at a table

**1. The wound clock and the printed career contradict each other, and neither chapter says so.** This is new information, produced by joining three rulings that landed in the last two days and had never been read together.

*The law, all printed.* A Wound is taken on **every** drop, whether or not you spend Grit to get back up (`13:355`). **Nothing gives one back** (`13:450`): not a long rest, not Medicine, not magic. **Four Wounds ends the hero** (`13:454`). So a hero's entire career is four drops to 0 HP, and Grit does not extend it: Grit is spent *after* the drop and does not prevent the Wound.

*The rate, measured two ways.* `scripts/heal-threshold-sim.py` puts a Standard fight at **4 Grit spends, therefore 4 Wound rolls, for a party of four**, and at every tier without a healer. `scripts/rounds-to-resolve.py`, which does not know the exit exists, independently puts a Standard fight at 73 / 81 / 71% of the party's pool, with the drop arriving at 4.2 / 3.7 / 3.6 rounds against fights of 3.79 / 3.73 / 3.27. Two instruments, one answer: **a Standard fight costs roughly one drop per hero.** Played rather than modelled, it is worse than that, because damage concentrates: in W-005's play of the book's own printed Adept Standard encounter, the hero the monsters focused spent **40 of their 56 pool, which is two drops**, while the other three spent 14 between them; and in W-003's played round, **two heroes hit 0 HP in a single round** (harmless then, two Wounds now).

*The arithmetic.* A party of four carries 16 Wounds of career between them. A Standard fight spends about four. **The party has roughly four Standard fights in it, and its front-line hero about two.** The book's own career pace is `18:23`: one level every two to three sessions, "a weekly group reaches level 10 in about half a year of play", with `22:274` printing a 44-52 DP career to match. Even granting that a session is not always a Standard fight, and that a healer halves the rate at Novice, the two printed tracks are out by several times, and the guidance chapter is the one that is wrong, because it presents Standard as the baseline rung a party should expect.

*What it costs a player.* A table that follows the book loses its Protector around the third session, in a game that has printed ten levels, and the DA's own tool is the trap: `19:53`'s "Standard = about three quarters of the day" is true of the day and silent about the career. The player loses the character they built for a campaign before they have learned to play them, and nothing on the sheet warned them.

*My position, and it is a balance call rather than a preference.* The clock is right and the guidance is silent. The Wound Table is the book's best mechanic, #774 is what makes a sheet a biography, and the exit is the paragraph I would defend hardest in the whole book; the defect is that the ladder is priced in staying power, which comes back, and never in Wounds, which do not. So the repair is not to move the exit (settled last cycle, and moving it again would be thrash) and not to restore a recovery channel (#774 is the ruling). It is to **price the ladder in the currency that lasts**: `19:53`'s rungs should say what each one costs in Wound rolls, and `18:23`'s pace sentence should say whether it describes one hero or a party that rotates. Reversal is one word: delete the sentence. Row 224 named this measurement as its revival condition and this review is that measurement run; the row records it.

**REPAIRED (cycle 58, 2026-09-27, head `2ec5a62`; issue #799, PR #800 squash-merged, audit attached).** The question in this item was an open fork and it is now closed with a measurement rather than a preference. `scripts/career-clock.py` prices every rung in Wounds and reads the career off the same numbers (selftest passing, calibrated against `heal-threshold-sim.py`'s validated Standard figure of 4 party Wounds): a Standard fight costs a party of four **4.0 Wounds at every tier, and the floor and ceiling readings agree at 4.0**, Easy a third of a Wound per hero, Hard one to two, Deadly two or more, which at Novice spends every Grit the party has. So the answer to the fork is **the printed pace describes the party, not one hero**: at 2.5 sessions a level its 25-session arc is **6.2x** longer than a hero's clock, and the book now says so at `18:23` while `19:55` prints each rung's price in the currency that does not come back. The dial that lets a table choose between a short bright career (about four Standard fights) and a long one (about twenty Easy ones on the same hero) is the rung mix the ladder already sells. No printed number moved, no rule was added, and #774 and the exit at four both stand; reversal is one word.

**2. A dedicated healer is a measured cost to the party from Adept up, and nothing says so.** The same instrument: with no healer a Standard fight costs the party 4 Wound rolls at every tier; with one healer it costs **2 at Novice, but 5 at Adept and 7 at Master**, because a healer's Action is an Action not spent attacking and the fight runs longer, so the extra rounds cost more than the healing returns. The instrument's own limit is stated in its file and matters: it models HP and drops, and cannot price the Wound roll a heal *prevents*. Still, the sign flips in the tier where campaigns actually live (Adept is levels 3-6), and it is the opposite of what the book tells the player: Divine Intervention says healing changes every fight. Harm at a table is a player building the role the book describes and quietly making the day worse, which is the failure mode a review exists to catch. The lever is precedent rather than invention, and the Life tree started down it this week: *Staunch* and *Set Bone* answer the Wound Table's lasting consequences, which is the axis where a healer's Action is unambiguously worth spending. Reversal: one word.

**3. 41 of 49 creatures still leave their target to the DA.** The five blocks where choosing wrong breaks the encounter now carry their cue (PR #751) and the frame is printed (`20:32`, plus `13:317` for the who-stops-fighting axis), so what remains is the honest residue of a companion book rather than a gap. The cost is a duller fight: a creature that hits whoever is nearest, every round, plays as a bag of numbers. I would not write 41 role lines; I would leave the frame and watch whether a second adventure's blocks come back needing more.

**4. The flagship teaching example is still not a level-1 example.** `01:144` is where a new reader learns what a roll looks like, and the pair in it are "seasoned adventurers, several levels into their careers" (`01:144`). The derivation works and the disclosure is honest. The cheapest lever is a pointer to the level-1 adventure, not a rewrite.

*Withdrawn by measurement (cycle 45) and still withdrawn.* **Adept is the tightest tier** was a real observation with no repair available: `19:53` already prints the ladder's cost at every tier and the resource model lands at 73.0 / 81.0 / 71.5%, a 6-point overshoot against the word "about" on a rounded guideline whose next rung is "the whole day". Item 2 above is the one thing this review adds to that picture, and it is a different claim: not that Adept is tight, but that a healer turns from gain to cost exactly there.

*Closed since the last review.* (a) The tactics primer the previous verdict ranked first landed (`13:85`, PR #784). (b) The wound arc has an ending (`13:454`, PR #783) and nothing heals a Wound (`13:450`, PR #775). (c) The healing law is printed in one place with its exclusions (`08:238-251`) and the eight cards it governs read against the current row (`12:95-140`: thresholds, not amounts). (d) The Life tree answers the Wound Table (`12:102` *Staunch*, `12:120` *Set Bone*, PR #780), and the rung-monotonicity gate that caught the one rung in it that lost half the heal its Weak promised is now a standing instrument. (e) The permanence sweep's one survivor is repaired (`19:486`, PR #786).

## 9. What is missing

- **The campaign clock.** Not broken on the page: absent. Nothing tells a DA what a career costs in the currency that does not come back, which is why section 8 item 1 is a surprise rather than a design. The book owes this at `19:53` more than it owed the exit at `13:454`, and it is the next thing I will build.
- **A second adventure, and a campaign frame.** The book ships one adventure, for level 1, and its closing hook (`19:373`) points at a next adventure that does not exist. Play stops at level 2 in printed material, and a level-7 table has no printed encounter to run. This is the largest hole in the game and it is not a repair. It is worth saying plainly that it is now a *larger* hole than it was: the game prints an ending for a hero and a ten-level ladder to spend before you reach it, and the material to spend it on does not exist.
- **A world chapter.** Thornwell, the Iron Circle, the Sunken Vault and the Argent Circle imply a setting the book never describes.
- **An answer for a healer at the top of the ladder.** The Life tree's tiers buy reach and targets (item 2 above); the axis that would make the Adept and Master rungs feel like upgrades is the one the new cards started on.

## 10. The verdict

*Heroes of Legend* is a playable, opinionated, fast fantasy RPG that has decided the to-hit roll is not worth its table time and built carefully around that decision: every attack lands, every spell fires, the players roll everything including their own defence, and falling costs a scar. It is for tables that want heroic sword and sorcery with short rounds and light bookkeeping, and for the DA who would rather narrate than roll. It is playable today, and this week it got a soul: the wound table became permanent and then got an ending, and the paragraph that ends a hero is the best writing in the book. Its new problem is the price of that paragraph, and the price is not printed.

**The single most valuable thing to do next is to put the career clock on the page**: price `19:53`'s rungs in Wound rolls rather than only in staying power, and make `18:23`'s pace sentence say whether it describes one hero or a party that rotates. It is a guidance-chapter repair, it needs no ruling reversed, it costs no printed number, and without it the book's newest and best mechanic is a trap rather than a design: a table following the baseline rung retires its front-liners around the third session and concludes that the game is broken, when in fact it is working exactly as written. After that the ceiling is unchanged and it is not a repair: printed play stops at level 2, and a table that finishes the starter adventure has nowhere the book tells it to go.
