# Places and People

A settlement becomes memorable when the heroes have a reason to return. Perhaps
the innkeeper knows their names, a workshop holds an unfinished commission, or
a friend waits for news. You can build that connection with a few people and
places before you know the region's ancient history.

HOL does not require a particular world. Decide with your group what magic looks
like, how distant communities exchange news, and which powers shape local life.
Keep those choices separate from rules that apply to every character.

## Begin within reach

Choose a home base, a nearby danger and a route between them. Add one person who
needs the heroes, one who can help them, and one whose interests may conflict with
theirs. Name the next destination only when it becomes useful.

Give each location an ordinary purpose. A ruin once stored grain, held prisoners,
watched a road or honored the dead. That purpose suggests entrances, rooms, tools
and paths. Its current inhabitants then change it. A flooded granary becomes a
fungus garden; a beacon tower becomes a smuggler's signal post.

| Location prompt | Questions that make it playable |
|---|---|
| A settlement | What brings people here? What do they need from elsewhere? Who settles disputes? |
| A route | Who travels it? What can be seen ahead? Where can travelers leave it? |
| A ruin | What was it built to do? What still works? Who now claims it? |
| A lair | How does its inhabitant enter, feed and escape? What warns an approaching visitor? |
| A refuge | Who offers shelter? What makes it safe now? What responsibilities accompany hospitality? |

## Draw for decisions

On a regional map, show routes, obstacles and recognizable landmarks. Exact miles
are unnecessary when they do not affect a choice. If two routes differ in travel
time, explain that difference along with what the heroes know about their risks.
Ordinary travel does not require routine rolls or an exhaustion track.

On a tactical map, use squares. Mark difficult terrain, actual barriers, attack
paths and usable exits. Partial obstruction applies 1 Bane; heavy cover with a
narrow attack path applies 3. An unseen target behind an opaque barrier is not
merely a heavily covered target. Abilities still require their own targeting conditions.

Give melee heroes approaches, ranged heroes sight lines, and movement abilities
places to matter. Avoid filling every battlefield with the same waist-high walls.
A bridge, orchard, crowded workshop and wind-scoured roof should suggest different
choices before a creature takes its first turn.

> **Example: the working mill.** The yard offers open movement. Sacks inside create
> difficult terrain; the mill wall blocks sight entirely. A doorway provides an
> attack path only where a target is visible through it. The wheel is machinery,
> not automatically an extra creature with a turn. If it presents a hazard, define
> that hazard's trigger and resolution before someone risks it.

## People you can portray quickly

Write four things for an important NPC: a recognizable detail, a current want, a
useful piece of knowledge, and a boundary they hesitate to cross. Add a name and
one relationship. You now have enough to answer most questions naturally.

| NPC | At the table | What changes their mind |
|---|---|---|
| A keeper of a roadside shrine | Repairs travelers' straps while listening; wants the road safe. | A practical plan for the missing pilgrims matters more than flattery. |
| A quarry foreman | Dust in every seam; knows where the newest tunnel broke through. | Will stop work if shown a real danger, but needs a way to pay the crew. |
| A local broker | Remembers favors precisely; knows which goods are scarce. | Accepts a useful introduction or an agreed trade, not imaginary coin value. |
| A young watch officer | Rehearses orders under their breath; fears being blamed. | Written evidence or a respected witness may resolve their uncertainty. |

These descriptions do not grant abilities, resistances or skill modifiers. If an
NPC needs a combat role, select a fitting existing creature card and describe the
person around it. If their important noncombat task needs mechanics, record its
attribute and applicable capability openly rather than inventing a hidden unbeatable
score after the player acts.

## Factions with room to change

A faction needs a shared purpose and a means to pursue it. Write what it wants,
what it can offer, what it can threaten, and where its members disagree. Rivalry
becomes interesting when the heroes can affect it.

Consider two groups competing over a flooded quarry. Workers want safe access
and paid wages. A shrine's custodians want the newly exposed burial chamber left
undisturbed. Both may oppose a merchant who hides evidence of the breach. The
heroes can help several people without accepting every demand from any one group.

Keep a short record of what each group learns about the heroes. Information must
travel. A distant faction does not automatically know a private decision made
in a sealed room. If a messenger carries the news, that person becomes a useful
part of the adventure.

## Cultures without cages

Culture describes learned customs and community experience; ancestry does not
force one culture, profession or loyalty. Let towns contain disagreement and
travelers carry habits from elsewhere. Ask players how their own origins fit
rather than assigning a personality from a character option.

Likewise, a deity, spirit or tradition can have a local name without changing a
card's action or healing cost. A priest and a scholar may describe Life energy
differently while using the same underlying rules. If you want a setting choice
to change a rule, discuss that change explicitly before character choices depend
on it.

## Let places remember

After an adventure, change a few visible details. The damaged bridge gains a new
rail. A market stall sells charms shaped like the creature the heroes drove away.
A family leaves a chair empty for someone who did not return. These signs make
choices matter without needing a regional simulation.

Return to people as their wants change. An employer may become a partner, a rescued
stranger a critic, a frightened rival a dependable guide. Keep their earlier actions
in view so the relationship grows from play rather than replacing itself each visit.
