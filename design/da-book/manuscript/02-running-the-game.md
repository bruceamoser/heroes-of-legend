# Running the Game

A player wants to slip past a watchman, pull a companion from a river, or convince
a frightened witness to speak. Begin with the intended action. The dice enter when
you know what is uncertain and why the answer matters.

The following procedure works in a crowded market as readily as in a dungeon.
Existing cards take precedence where they provide a specific action and outcome.

## From intent to consequence

Ask what the hero wants and how they attempt it. Establish what they can perceive,
what their action can accomplish, and what happens if they make only limited
progress. Give the player this information before they commit. They may choose a
different approach once they understand the risk.

If the action plainly works, let it work. Lifting a rug reveals the trapdoor beneath
it. Walking across a sound bridge does not require a balance roll. If an attempt
cannot work, explain the obstacle: the gate is beyond reach, the witness does not
know the answer, or the chosen ability cannot target what the hero cannot see.

When uncertainty carries a consequence, call for an attribute or use the attribute
printed on the declared card. The six attributes are **Brawn, Fortitude, Agility,
Guile, Knowledge and Reason**. Do not add a skill bonus or replace an ability's
attribute because another attribute is stronger. A **Force action** calls for
Brawn; “Force” is not a seventh kind of roll.

A player declares a card before rolling. Afterward, resolve the chosen action's
outcome. An unexpected low result is not permission to switch to another card.

## Difficulty without changing the target

Roll 3d6 plus the named attribute. **Weak** is 8 or below, **Standard** is 9–14,
and **Strong** is 15 or above. Difficulty changes the dice through **Boons and
Banes**, not these thresholds.

Boons and Banes cancel one for one. Roll one extra die for each remaining Boon or
Bane, to a maximum of three extra dice. With Boons, keep the highest three; with
Banes, keep the lowest three. Add the attribute to those three dice. Three retained
natural 1s produce Weak plus a complication; three retained natural 6s produce
Strong plus a benefit.

Use a single Bane for a meaningful obstacle, two for a severe obstacle and three
for an exceptional obstacle that remains possible. Apply Boons in the same way
for useful advantages. These descriptions help you make consistent judgments;
they do not turn an impossible action into a difficult roll. Do not count the
same circumstance twice under different names.

> **Example: the rain-slick ledge.** A hero follows a narrow ledge under pressure.
> You assign 1 Bane for its slippery footing. An ally supplies plausible Aid for
> 1 Boon. They cancel, leaving the ordinary three dice. The ledge does not also
> change Standard to 12 or add an extra numerical penalty.

## Weak is progress

A **consequence** is what the action puts at stake. A **complication** introduces
an additional problem without taking away progress already earned. A **benefit**
adds an advantage. State the ordinary outcome before rolling; add a complication
or benefit when the rules or the circumstances call for one.

An ability already tells you what its Weak, Standard and Strong results do. Do
not replace Weak damage with a miss, or substitute an invented drawback for the
card's printed result. Outside a card's specific procedure, agree on what progress
means in the current situation.

> **Example: persuade the ferryman.** The ferryman refuses to cross while the bell
> at the far landing sounds an alarm. A hero asks him to reveal what he saw. You
> establish that Weak gets an account from shore, Standard persuades him to guide
> them along the bank, and Strong also earns the use of his boat once the alarm is
> resolved. The roll cannot make him know who rang the bell. A natural 111 might
> add that another listener reports the conversation; his account remains gained.

If another attempt would be identical and could be repeated without cost, danger
or change, summarize the work instead of asking for rolls until someone gets
Strong. Where an attempt consumes meaningful time, state how much time and what
may change. Let a new approach change the circumstances rather than charging a
roll simply for the privilege of trying again.

## Information, perception and hiding

Describe obvious information freely. When a consequential search is uncertain,
call for **Reason**. When someone might perceive a concealed player character,
that player rolls **Agility** to remain Hidden. Do not add an opposed observer roll.
A hidden hero needs plausible concealment; a good die result cannot hide them in
an empty, brightly lit room.

**Hidden** is a universal state. Do not maintain a separate hidden marker for each
observer. Attacking or openly revealing oneself ends it. Judge movement by the
actual concealment available. **Invisible** prevents sight but does not necessarily
conceal location: footprints in ash, a ringing bell or a moving curtain still matter.
Active searching or attempting to hide during combat costs a Main Action unless
a card says otherwise.

For an unscripted search or concealment roll, say what each outcome can achieve in
that scene. Do not assume every Weak search finds nothing, or that every Weak
stealth roll grants Hidden. It might reveal the direction of a sound while leaving
its source unknown, or reach shelter while leaving a visible trail. These are
scene rulings, not new universal outcome tables.

Never put every route to an essential clue behind one roll. If players inspect the
thing that contains the answer, give them the answer. Other rolls can determine
how quickly they understand it, what else they discover, or who notices them.

## Work together outside combat

A **group check** combines several heroes' efforts outside combat. Name the task,
who can meaningfully contribute, its target, the time per attempt and its consequence.
State whether the task is **binary**, which must reach its target in one attempt,
or **cumulative**, which retains progress between attempts.

Each participant rolls an appropriate attribute and contributes 1/2/3 progress
on Weak/Standard/Strong. A hero can contribute their own roll or provide Aid to
another participant, not both to the same attempt. Set the target for the actual
task and space available. Do not quietly raise it whenever another helper joins.

> **Example: the gate before the patrol.** Three heroes can get a shoulder beneath
> the swollen gate. The task is binary, target 7, one attempt before the patrol
> rounds the bend. Weak, Standard and Strong total 6: the gate stays closed and the
> patrol arrives. Weak, Strong and Strong total 7: it opens. The 6 does not carry
> into the next attempt. Hauling a boulder uphill could instead retain its progress
> because the task is cumulative.

A three-person attempt yields 3–9 progress before special effects. Use that range
to avoid accidentally assigning an unreachable target. A target represents the
amount of combined work, not a new difficulty threshold for each roll.
Group checks do not apply to combat, crafting, recipe derivation or wound treatment.
In combat, a hero can spend their own Force action on an obstacle under its normal
rules; the group does not pool simultaneous rolls.

## Conversation is an action in the world

Give an NPC a want, a concern and a limit. Let the players speak in character or
summarize a proposal. Use the attribute printed on a social card. Without one,
choose the attribute that matches the approach: Guile for a deceptive bargain,
Knowledge for a technical case, or Reason to untangle conflicting accounts.

Good evidence may settle a question without a roll. A roll can change how a
witness cooperates, but cannot make them reveal a fact they never knew. Likewise,
a bargain does not erase another person's responsibilities or compel a player
character's beliefs. Apply an explicit supernatural ability according to its card;
ordinary persuasion does not acquire that ability by a high result.

> **Example: the missing wage chest.** A guard suspects the heroes of theft. Showing
> the recovered chest and its unbroken seal answers that suspicion. Do not require
> a second persuasion roll just to make the guard notice the seal. Negotiating
> access to a restricted archive afterward is a different request.

## Enter turns when order matters

Use combat turns when competing actions, immediate danger and short durations need
an exact order. At the start, roll unmodified 3d6 once: 12 or above means the
players' side starts; below 12 means the opposing side starts. Alternate one actor
from each side. Each side chooses its next actor. When one side has no unacted
members, the other finishes its remaining turns. The same side starts each round;
do not reroll initiative.

Each actor has one Main Action, one Maneuver, one Reaction and movement. Main
converts to Maneuver, and Maneuver to movement; conversion never goes upward.
An ordinary defense roll does not spend the defender's Reaction. A shield hunker
or an opportunity attack does.

Starting a provoking action triggers an available opportunity attack before that
action resolves. It uses **Basic Melee**, not a specialty card. Resolve it, then
continue the original action if its actor remains capable. Damage alone does not
cancel a spell or other action. Leaving melee voluntarily, casting beside an enemy,
standing, stowing and retrieving items can provoke. Forced movement does not.
Drawing a weapon as part of its attack remains part of that action.

> **Example: a healer under pressure.** A hero next to an enemy begins casting a
> healing spell. The enemy spends its Reaction on Basic Melee. If the hero remains
> capable and can pay the healing cost while staying above 0 HP, the spell proceeds.
> If the attack drops the hero to 0, they cannot continue. There is no extra
> concentration or counterspell roll.

## Who handles the dice

| Situation | Roll and result |
|---|---|
| A player character attacks | The player rolls the attack; read attack outcomes. Even against another PC, do not add a defense roll. |
| A creature attacks a PC | The player rolls the named defense attribute; read the creature's defense outcomes. |
| A creature uses an area attack against PCs | Each affected player rolls their own defense. |
| A player uses an area attack | Roll once using the worst applicable target modifiers. |
| A player-controlled summon acts or defends | Its controller rolls the summon's printed attribute and uses the appropriate player reference. |
| An NPC attacks an NPC | A player or the DA assuming that role rolls the defender's named attribute; read the attacker's defense outcomes. |
| An NPC heals or performs another consequential task | A player or the DA rolls the acting NPC's printed attribute and outcomes. |

Apply ordinary modifiers in each case. Rolling for an NPC does not transfer control
of that NPC. Events well away from the heroes can be resolved by your judgment
without simulating a battle nobody is playing.

## Return to the scene

When immediate opposition ends, resolve remaining urgent consequences before
moving on. A Dying character still needs their survival procedure. Burning can be
put out when companions have the time and means to do so; combat need not continue
merely to count safe, uncontested extinguishing actions. A lasting wound, curse or
persistent condition remains until its own remedy applies.

Once the heroes can safely take a breath, restore full HP. Preserve Grit expenditure,
scars and other continuing effects. Ask what they do with the scene they have
changed: whom they help, what they search, and whether they stay or leave.
