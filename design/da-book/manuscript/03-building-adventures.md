# Building Adventures

An adventure begins with something that cannot stay as it is. A bridge has closed,
a tomb has opened, or two allies have made promises that cannot both be kept.
Give the heroes a reason to care and a chance to change the outcome.

Prepare the people, places and pressures around that problem. Leave the heroes'
solutions to their players. A useful adventure can survive a bargain you did not
expect, an avoided fight, or a villain captured before delivering a speech.

## Start with the problem

Write one sentence naming what is wrong and who is affected. Then write what
happens if nobody intervenes. These statements give the adventure direction
without deciding the players' actions.

“The village is threatened by monsters” gives you little to run. “The quarry has
broken into an occupied burial hall, and its guardians now prevent the workers
from reaching the only road out” gives you people, a place, a cause and several
possible solutions. The heroes might close the breach, learn the guardians'
orders, find another road or fight their way through.

Give the problem a visible face. A frightened apprentice arrives with a broken
lantern. The ferryman has tied his boat ashore. An old rival asks for help and
cannot afford to be proud. The opening should invite a decision the players can
make with the information they have.

## Offer reasons, not a single hook

Tie the situation to more than one interest. Coin can draw one hero; a missing
friend draws another; a rare recipe interests a third. Ask players where their
characters connect to the problem. Their answers can replace a disposable employer
with someone the company already cares about.

Keep the offered terms clear. If a patron promises payment, say what counts as
completion. “Return my brother alive” and “reopen the crossing” are different
contracts. If those goals later conflict, that conflict should come from the
situation, not from a hidden change to the agreement.

## Make opposition want something

An adversary's purpose tells you how they act when the plan goes wrong. A robber
wants supplies. A sentinel preserves a boundary. A rival expedition wants credit
for a discovery. These motives supply ways to bargain, distract, bypass or defeat
them without inventing a special peaceful solution for every scene.

For each important adversary, note what they know, what they fear losing, and what
would make them retreat or negotiate. An ancestry is not a motive. Two goblins may
have different loyalties; a ghost may understand an offer a living guard rejects.
Creature cards supply useful starting purposes and counterplay, not compulsory
personalities for every individual.

## Build places that connect

Sketch the adventure as connected locations before you draw detailed squares.
A crossing might link a ferry house, an old causeway, a toll yard and a buried
signal room. Give important places more than one route where the geography allows
it. Decide which routes are obvious, which can be discovered, and what choosing
each route costs in time, danger or access.

At each location, prepare a short arrival description, what is happening, what
can be learned, and what changes when the heroes interfere. Add a battle map only
where positioning might matter. Show exits, cover, obstacles and hazards clearly.
A secret route should lead somewhere useful, not merely skip a paragraph.

## Clues should connect the adventure

For every fact the heroes need, provide several plausible ways to learn it. A
signal's meaning might appear in a keeper's log, in a diagram by the bell, and in
the ferryman's memory. These are separate opportunities, not three required rolls.

Distinguish finding evidence from interpreting it. Reading a legible name does not
need a roll. An identification card may reveal properties of an enchanted object
that ordinary inspection cannot. A Reason roll under pressure may reveal a hurried
alteration before the author returns. Give each procedure the work it actually does.

If the heroes miss a clue, let the world continue. Another witness may approach,
the threat may expose itself, or the group may choose a less informed route.
Preserve what their earlier decisions changed. Do not silently move every missed
clue into the next room until only one interpretation is possible.

## Pressure you can explain

Choose events that follow from time or action. A caravan leaves at dusk. Water
reaches a weakened foundation after the rain. A lookout runs for help if allowed
to escape. Tell the players what their heroes can recognize about these dangers.

Use a short sequence of likely developments, not a secret timer that advances
whenever you want tension. If the heroes stop the messenger, the reinforcements
should not arrive by an unexplained alternative route. If an event's timing is
uncertain, decide and record it before the choice that depends upon it.

| Pressure | Visible sign | A way to change it |
|---|---|---|
| Workers prepare to abandon a site | Carts are being loaded; a foreman announces departure. | Secure the road or persuade them a workable rescue is underway. |
| A rival takes the same route | Familiar bootprints cross the fresh mud. | Travel faster, negotiate, or choose another entrance. |
| A dangerous machine keeps operating | Its cycle is audible and its controls are visible. | Stop its power, operate the controls, or pass during a safe interval. |
| A captive is being moved | A witness reports preparations and the destination. | Intercept the transfer or arrange access at its destination. |

## Mix the kinds of choices

An investigation needs people who know different parts of the truth. A rescue
needs a person in danger and a route that can carry them out. An expedition needs
places worth discovering and a reason to choose one route over another. A siege
needs supplies, loyalties and exits as well as enemies.

Look at your party's abilities when deciding what those places contain. A tracker
needs readable signs. A negotiator needs someone able to agree. A crafter needs
materials, recipes or damaged objects worth understanding. Give these opportunities
real consequences; do not confine utility abilities to decorative scenes between
important fights.

Let an ability bypass a problem it is designed to solve. A hero who invested in
that capability should feel the difference. The rest of the adventure can respond
to their success without taking it away.

## End with a changed situation

An adventure can finish when the crossing opens, the prisoner is safe, or the truth
becomes public. It need not finish with every opponent dead. Settle promised rewards,
show who benefited, and ask what the heroes do with what they learned.

Record unresolved elements as future possibilities: the employer who lied, the
rival spared, the recipe found without its rare ingredient. Avoid turning every
success into a concealed disaster. A rescued person can simply be grateful.
