# Languages in Your World

Give languages places to live. A river port may speak three, while a remote valley
uses a local language and a handful of trade phrases. An old bridge may carry a
warning in a language nobody nearby reads. These details invite the heroes to
listen, find help and form relationships.

Use the shared language list as a ready starting point. You do not need to invent
a family tree of every language before the first session. Decide what the party
shares, which communities they are likely to meet, and one unfamiliar language
that can matter to an early adventure.

## Set expectations before creation

Tell players which name replaces Common, which ordinary choices are useful near
the starting location, and whether any normally uncommon language is ordinary in
this campaign. A port where dragons trade openly might make Draconic ordinary;
an isolated kingdom might have no living speakers of it. Explain that difference
before someone spends a language grant.

The starting baseline is Common plus one ordinary home language, with explicit
extra grants added afterward. If your world uses a different baseline, agree on
it before creation and preserve meaningful choices from language-granting traits.
Do not invalidate a selected language merely because the first prepared adventure
uses another one. Give its speakers, documents or contacts a place in the campaign.

Language follows upbringing and experience. A dwarf raised in an elven community
can speak Elven; a human scholar can know Dwarven. A culture entry is a way of life,
not a single nation with one compulsory language. Deep Halls might use Dwarven,
a regional language or several languages in different settlements.

## Add or replace a language

Write a short entry with the following information. Keep this record beside your
campaign notes and share the player-relevant choices before they become purchases.

| Record | Decide |
|---|---|
| Name and scope | One language, not every language of a species or continent. |
| Speakers and places | Who uses it, where they learned it, and whom they trade with. |
| Availability | Ordinary or uncommon in this campaign; which existing grants can choose it. |
| Form and script | Spoken, signed or another explicit form; whether it has writing and what is needed to use it. |
| Related varieties | Which dialects are mutually understood, and which are separate languages. |
| Adventure use | One conversation, document, contact or discovery that rewards knowing it. |

Do not give a language an inherent Boon to persuasion or a damage bonus. Its value
is access to people and meaning. If you want a magical language that compels a
listener or opens a sealed gate, put that supernatural effect in an ability, item
or specific adventure feature with its own rules.

> **Example: Reed Speech.** The fishing communities of the southern marsh use an
> ordinary spoken language written on split reed strips. Neighboring village
> dialects are mutually understood. A lost island dialect is not automatically a
> separate choice: decide whether it is still understandable before preparing a
> translation obstacle. A ferryman's account in Reed Speech reveals which boat
> crossed after sunset. Nothing about the language requires its speakers to share
> ancestry or allegiance.

## Use barriers to create choices

An unfamiliar language should suggest a next step. The party might hire an
interpreter, visit a shrine's archive, find a bilingual inscription or use Translate
the Fragment. Let the players choose which cost or relationship to accept.
A necessary clue should have another way forward if nobody chose its language.
The specialist can obtain a clearer, faster or richer answer without making the
rest of the adventure depend on one choice made at creation.

Do not call for a roll just to understand ordinary speech in a known language.
If the uncertainty concerns a damaged inscription, a lie or a coded message, name
that problem and its consequences. A Knowledge roll does not confer an unknown
language. Where a task supports progress, use the existing procedure and the
applicable card rather than inventing a separate translation die.

An interpreter has knowledge, motives and limits like any other person. Establish
what they can actually translate. Routine honest interpretation needs no second
roll for every sentence. If deception or misunderstanding matters, show evidence
and use the normal player-roll rules. Never replace a card's explicit shared-language
requirement with an unannounced exception.

Treat dialects as mutually understandable unless a meaningful setting distinction
was established. A strange accent is usually flavor. Signed languages are complete
languages, not a bundle of secret combat signals: they require visible gestures
and follow the same rules for understanding. Ordinary gestures remain available
to everyone for simple meanings.

## Read the bestiary's communication line

The communication line states what that creature can do. “Understands” does not
mean “speaks.” “None” does not mean it fails to react to posture, food or danger;
it means the card grants no linguistic communication. A creature's ability to
communicate also does not guarantee its willingness to negotiate.

| Existing entry | Prepare it for your world |
|---|---|
| Common and its community's language | Name one local language before play. Goblin, Orc or Kobold may fit, but community matters more than ancestry. |
| Court, regional or scholarly language | Select one actual language from your campaign; these are not grants of every language in that category. |
| Sacred languages or language of its oath | Name the languages tied to that being's tradition. Celestial is one possible choice, not a compulsory one. |
| Old woodland speech | Use Elven or Fey, or name a distinct local language and decide its availability. |
| Languages known in life | Choose the dead person's languages. Being undead grants no universal language of the dead. |
| Maker's language | Name the maker's language and retain any “understands” or “repeats” limitation. |
| Images, calls, gestures or light | Convey only the meanings the card allows; do not silently add speech, mind-reading or perfect translation. |

Record concrete choices beside the creature entry in your adventure notes. When
several creatures share a community, reuse that choice consistently. Summoned forms
retain their printed communication, but commands follow the summon rules: an
uncommanded summon does nothing. Language never supplies free commands or extra actions.

## Learning during a campaign

Honor permanent language grants already printed on a trait or ability. For another
route to fluency, agree on its source and cost before the hero commits resources.
The core catalog currently has no universal Language Study purchase or fixed
training calendar. Do not disguise either as an existing rule. You can introduce
a clearly described campaign option or grant, but distinguish it from a translation,
a tutor's service or a temporary magical effect.

Keep the reward visible. A letter understood, a name pronounced correctly or a
conversation without an intermediary can show the benefit more clearly than an
extra modifier. Languages give your world voices; the rules need only make those
voices usable at the table.
