# Becoming the Dungeon Architect

Rain runs down the broken gate. Beyond it, a lantern moves where no living guard
should stand. One hero reaches for a weapon; another recognizes the lantern's
signal. You describe the light, answer their questions, and ask what they do.
Their answer is where the adventure begins.

As the **Dungeon Architect**, or **DA**, you portray the world beyond the heroes.
You give its inhabitants purposes, reveal what the characters can perceive, and
apply the rules when an uncertain choice has consequences. The players decide
what their heroes attempt. Together, your decisions turn a prepared situation
into a story none of you could have written alone.

You do not need to prepare every road beyond the gate. You need to know who holds
the lantern, what they want, and what might happen when someone answers it.

## A world worth entering

Give the players something they can act upon. A ruined watchtower becomes useful
when its beacon is still burning, someone is trapped beneath it, and the only
visible stairs have collapsed. Describe a few distinct details, then let questions
bring the rest into focus. A smell of wet ash or a voice behind the wall can do
more work than a page of history.

Tell players what their characters would reasonably know. A local knows which
bridge charges a toll. A healer recognizes ordinary signs of injury. A hero
standing beside a roaring furnace can feel its heat. Do not hide obvious danger
behind a roll, or withhold ordinary knowledge until someone uses the right phrase.
Specialist abilities matter when the task calls for their expertise or their
printed benefits.

Make consequences follow established circumstances. A guard who heard shouting
may investigate. A merchant whose stock was saved may offer favorable terms. Once
the group learns these connections, preparation and clever choices become useful.
They can trust that a door stays unlocked after they unlock it.

## Before the first session

Bring the players into the conversation before they finish their characters.
Describe the kind of adventures you intend to offer: expeditions into forgotten
places, dangerous roads, struggles between rival powers, or some mixture. Ask
what each hero hopes to accomplish and why these people would travel together.
A shared debt, a rescue, a commission or a promise can bring very different heroes
onto the same road.

Discuss the game's lasting injuries and death. HP can return after a safe breath;
spent Grit and wounds remain. Agree on how much detail the group wants when those
injuries occur. A player can prefer a brief statement of a wound's effects without
changing those effects. Establish a simple way for anyone to pause or redirect a
scene when its content makes play uncomfortable.

Set practical expectations as well: session length, attendance, how the group
handles an absent player, and whether players enjoy speaking in character or
summarizing their intent. Both approaches work. A player's confidence as a speaker
should not determine whether their silver-tongued hero gets to use an ability.

| Prepare | A useful first-session answer |
|---|---|
| A shared reason to act | Someone the heroes care about is overdue at a nearby crossing. |
| A place to begin | A crowded ferry house with news, supplies and a disputed map. |
| A visible problem | The crossing's signal has changed, and the ferryman refuses to sail. |
| People with different wants | One wants a rescue; another wants the road reopened quietly. |
| Several approaches | Follow the bank, bargain for passage, or inspect the old causeway. |
| A possible opposition | A few creature cards whose motives fit the place. |
| A consequence of delay | The stranded travelers move to a less safe shelter. |

This is enough to begin. Keep the names, nearby locations and creature cards within
reach. Leave room to discover what the players consider important.

## The rhythm of Heroes of Legend

HOL puts much of its strategy into character development. At the table, a player
chooses an action, declares the card they are using before the roll, and follows
its result. Your job is to establish the circumstances that make those choices
interesting: a narrow escape, a crowded bridge, an ally who needs a free hand.

Attacks always hit. Defensive play changes the outcome and the damage suffered;
it does not create a routine miss. Since damage is only part of an attack, pay
attention to the push that opens a route, the condition that changes a plan, or
the maneuver that allows someone else to act safely.

A safe breath restores HP. This lets the next encounter begin with heroes ready
to act, while wounds and spent Grit preserve the cost of earlier danger. Do not
assume a campaign needs a fixed number of fights between rests. An encounter can
matter because it costs time, exposes a secret, changes an alliance, or leaves a
scar, even when everyone later returns to full HP.

## Share the spotlight

Watch who is making decisions. After one player negotiates with the watch captain,
turn to the scout studying the courtyard or the healer tending a frightened
witness. Invite a contribution without requiring every hero to roll in every scene.
Sometimes the right contribution is a question or a choice to trust a companion.

Read the characters' less frequently used cards during preparation. Put opportunities
for them in the world: tracks worth following, a wound that needs proper treatment,
an object whose history can be discovered. Present the opportunity clearly. A
player should not need to guess that you prepared a scene for their expertise.

Let a good plan work. If the heroes bring the documents an official needs, obtain
permission and take the safe road, do not move the ambush onto that road merely
to preserve a fight. Preparation earns its value through what it changes.

## When the unexpected happens

Pause long enough to understand the plan. Determine whether it can work, whether
an existing card covers it, and what makes its outcome uncertain. If the players
have solved the problem through their actions, describe the result. If danger or
uncertainty remains, establish the stakes and call for the appropriate roll.

You can make a consistent ruling without designing a permanent subsystem. Explain
it briefly, apply it to comparable situations, and note a question for later
review if it is likely to recur. If a mistaken ruling materially changes a player's
decision, offer to revisit that decision before proceeding.

## Close the session with something to carry forward

Ask what the heroes intend to do next. Record people helped or harmed, promises,
new information, treasure and unresolved threats. Confirm wounds, treatment progress,
Grit and supplies with the players. Their plans tell you what to prepare; the
consequences tell you what the world does while they prepare.

a small place, a solvable problem and several people with reasons to care. Use it
whole or borrow the pieces that suit your table.
