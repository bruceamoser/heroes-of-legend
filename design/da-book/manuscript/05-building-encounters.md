# Building Encounters

An encounter asks the heroes to make a consequential choice. A fight might decide
who holds a bridge, whether a prisoner escapes, or how much danger the company
accepts to recover a lost object. Begin with that purpose. Then choose creatures
and a place that make the decision interesting.

**Combat Value**, or **CV**, helps compare creatures and assemble an initial roster.
It does not account perfectly for a particular party, map or combination of abilities.
The budgets in this chapter are playtest starting points. They have not yet been
calibrated to promise a particular fight length or chance of injury.

## Prepare the encounter in six decisions

1. **Choose the objective.** What do the heroes want, and what does the opposition want?
2. **Review the party.** Note actual levels, attack options, healing, control, mobility,
   supplies, wounds and available Grit.
3. **Choose an initial CV budget.** Account for party size, level and intended danger.
4. **Select a roster.** Give its members useful roles and understandable reasons to act.
5. **Draw the relevant terrain.** Show approaches, exits, sight lines and hazards.
6. **Decide what can end the encounter.** Include retreat, surrender or a completed
   objective where the situation allows them.

Write down all creatures that may join before the heroes can safely recover. A
reinforcement wave is part of the danger, even if it begins off the map.

## Starting budgets

For four heroes of the same level, the current standard seed is **32 + 8 × level**.
Use the row below as a first estimate, then examine the actual matchup.

| Level | Standard CV seed |
|---:|---:|
| 1 | 40 |
| 2 | 48 |
| 3 | 56 |
| 4 | 64 |
| 5 | 72 |
| 6 | 80 |
| 7 | 88 |
| 8 | 96 |
| 9 | 104 |
| 10 | 112 |

| Intended difficulty | Multiply the standard budget by | Preparation guidance |
|---|---:|---|
| Easy | 0.75 | Let a useful plan or favorable matchup show the heroes' strengths. |
| Standard | 1 | Begin with ordinary roles and modest terrain. |
| Hard | 1.5 | Signal the danger and review the roster's combined abilities carefully. |
| Extreme | 2 | Treat as a deliberate major risk; make its stakes and alternatives clear. |

These labels describe an intended test, not an established prediction. Doubling a
budget does not establish that a fight will last twice as long. In particular,
more disabling effects can remove player choices before they noticeably increase
round count.

For **N same-level heroes**, multiply the four-hero budget by **N ÷ 4**, then by
the difficulty multiplier. Round the final result to a convenient whole CV.

> **Example: a smaller company.** Three level-1 heroes have an initial standard
> budget of 40 × 3 ÷ 4 = **30 CV**. Five level-1 heroes have **50 CV**. A hard
> encounter for those five starts at 50 × 1.5 = **75 CV**.

For **mixed levels**, calculate each hero's share of the four-hero budget, add
those shares, then apply difficulty: **sum((32 + 8 × each hero's level) ÷ 4)**.
Do not round the party's average level first.

> **Example: experienced companions.** Heroes at levels 1, 1, 3 and 3 contribute
> 10 + 10 + 14 + 14 = **48 CV** before difficulty. A hard seed is 72. Review the
> lower-level heroes' vulnerability separately; the total does not protect them
> from an attack designed to pressure the stronger members.

A roster need not equal the budget exactly. A sensible 36-CV scene is more useful
than an unrelated extra creature added merely to reach 40.

## Give each creature a job

Start with a few creatures whose actions you can run confidently. Three to five
enemies make a useful first test against four heroes. Their roles should help
you choose actions, not grant unprinted bonuses.

| Role in this encounter | What to look for on its card | A decision it creates |
|---|---|---|
| Hold a route | Resistance, Defend, suitable positioning | Go around, displace it, or commit attacks? |
| Pressure from range | A printed ranged action and a usable sight line | Take cover or close the distance? |
| Separate companions | Push, pull, mobility or grappling | Recover formation or exploit another opening? |
| Disrupt a plan | A printed condition or interference ability | Protect the affected ally or remove the source? |
| Sustain allies | A printed healing ability with its cost | Pressure the healer or overwhelm another target? |
| Protect an objective | Motive and terrain that reward staying nearby | Fight it directly or complete the task elsewhere? |

Begin by adding one unusual capability at a time. Several enemies that repeatedly
apply Dazed or Paralyzed can create a scene in which players spend much of their
time unable to participate. Test that combination deliberately before presenting
it as an ordinary fight.

A creature with several attacks chooses among them using its normal action budget.
A claw and a bite do not grant two Main Actions. A solo has one turn and one
Reaction; there are no automatic legendary turns. Objectives and terrain can make
a solo memorable, but they do not secretly supply extra actions. Any additional
creatures contribute their own CV.

## Terrain changes the matchup

A ranged enemy in an unreachable position can be far more dangerous than the same
enemy in a room with several approaches. A narrow doorway can prevent the heroes
from using their numbers. A deep drop can turn modest forced movement into the
most important part of an attack.

Inspect the map from the players' side. Can they reach the enemy? Can they see it?
Can they retreat? Can an ally get beside someone who needs stabilization? Does a
large creature fit through the route you expect it to use? Check its actual footprint,
not just the miniature you happen to own.

Show meaningful features before the choice that depends on them. A ledge can be
dangerous without being hidden. A loose bridge rail should look loose. If a damaging
hazard operates during combat, record its additional pressure separately; the
creature budget does not already include it.

## Five encounters to begin with

These scenes use current creature CVs. Each presents a motive and a route to an
outcome other than defeating every enemy. Their totals are examples, not certified
encounter ratings.

| Encounter | Roster | Total CV |
|---|---|---:|
| The false toll | 3 [Road Bandits](../../bestiary/creatures/road-bandit.md), 1 [Bandit Bow](../../bestiary/creatures/bandit-bow.md) | 37 |
| The opened barrow | 3 [Skeleton Sentries](../../bestiary/creatures/bone-sentry.md), 2 [Skeleton Archers](../../bestiary/creatures/bone-archer.md) | 38 |
| Tracks around the camp | 4 [Wolves](../../bestiary/creatures/wolf.md) | 36 |
| The bridge has an owner | 1 [Bridge Troll](../../bestiary/creatures/bridge-troll.md), 1 Road Bandit, 1 Bandit Bow | 43 |
| The watch will not stand down | 2 [Shield Soldiers](../../bestiary/creatures/shield-soldier.md), 1 [Grave Cultist](../../bestiary/creatures/grave-cultist.md) | 43 |

**The false toll.** A cart blocks the road, but its axle is sound. The robbers want
supplies without a dangerous fight. Put the bow where heroes can reach or outflank
it. Inspecting the supposed breakdown exposes the lie; negotiation, an alternate
route or a forceful advance can settle the scene. The full crossing adventure
provides a worked version.

**The opened barrow.** The guardians repeat an old boundary command. A stolen burial
token explains why the entrance has become dangerous; returning it can settle this
particular disturbance if you establish that relationship in the adventure. That
is local story context, not a universal skeleton weakness. Leave room around the
entry so the encounter does not become a one-square queue.

**Tracks around the camp.** The camp obstructs the pack's route to its den. Tracks
and anxious circling reveal the problem. The wolves use their printed pack ability
against isolated prey. Moving camp or clearing a passage may prevent the fight.
Do not grant every animal in the bestiary the same pack ability.

**The bridge has an owner.** The troll and robbers share an uneasy toll arrangement.
Food, tribute or turning them against one another may open the way. Show the drop
and the troll's push capability through signs or observation before the party
commits. Fire counterplay can change the fight substantially; the 43-CV total does
not price a lethal fall or inaccessible firing position.

**The watch will not stand down.** Soldiers believe the cultist has lawful orders.
A forged seal, an inconsistent instruction or a credible witness can break that
belief. If a fight occurs, track the soldiers' separate Defend and Shield Hunker
costs and durations. Their layered defenses can make this harder for some parties
than its total suggests.

## Healing, flight and summoned allies

Healing follows the same rules for players and ordinary NPCs. A healer pays the
printed HP cost before resolution and must remain above 0. Free hands, range,
actions, Life resistance and opportunity attacks matter. Give the party visible
information about enemy healing rather than silently increasing its cost or limiting
it to a number of uses.

A flying creature needs the flight actually printed on its card. Review its ability
to attack while staying out of reach and what the party can do about that. Do not
assume every party has a ranged solution because bows exist in the equipment list.
Place cover, objectives or approaches that make the encounter playable, or present
the creature as a danger to avoid until prepared.

Summons consume their controller's commands. An uncommanded summon does nothing;
it does not provide a free guard, scout or opportunity attack. Its six-square
tether, inability to receive healing and disappearance when its controller falls
all affect its usefulness. Count what it can actually do, not an imaginary independent
turn. Do not automatically increase the budget to erase a summoner's investment.

## Observe, then adjust the estimate

For playtests, record the actual party and supplies rather than an idealized average
hero. Note rounds, healing actions and HP costs, Grit spent, new wounds, depleted
supplies, escapes, disabled turns, and whether players discovered the intended
counterplay. Record whether the encounter was enjoyable as well as whether the
company survived.

The design targets are roughly four rounds for a typical encounter, six for a hard
one and ten for an extreme one. A reasonably prepared party's typical encounter
is intended to have about a 20–30% chance of at least one new wound without a healer,
or 5–15% with one. These are goals across many trials, not requirements for an
individual table or evidence that this budget achieves them.

If an encounter disappoints, identify the cause before changing all its numbers.
Was the enemy unreachable? Did the party overlook a capability because it was
unclear? Did control remove too many turns? Did both sides spend their time healing?
Revise the relevant part and test again. Preserve what made the encounter memorable.
