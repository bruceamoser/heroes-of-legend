# Hazards, Traps and Environmental Challenges

A chain clicks inside the wall. Hot air escapes between the stones. A newly repaired
board stands out from the older floor. A good hazard gives the heroes something
to notice, a consequence to understand, and more than one way to respond.

Use existing actions, attributes, conditions and progress rules to resolve these
challenges. A trap does not need a second difficulty system. A hazardous place does
not need a combat turn merely because it can hurt someone.

**Review status:** the example hazards below are new content. Their damage, targets,
areas and timings are proposed values for these entries only. They are not universal
trap damage or calibrated additions to encounter CV.

## Write a hazard for use

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Signs | What can be seen, heard, smelled or learned before contact? |
| Trigger and area | What activates it, and which creatures or squares are affected? |
| Resolution | Is there a roll? Which attribute? What does each outcome do? |
| Timing and repetition | Does it happen once, repeat on a stated event, or continue until stopped? |
| Counterplay | How can the heroes avoid, interrupt or remove it? |
| Aftermath | What conditions, damage or changed terrain remain? |

An attack-like hazard uses player defense outcomes: Weak is the most dangerous,
Strong the least. Each affected player rolls for an area hazard. An active attempt
to repair or disable something instead uses the acting character's attribute and
progress. Label the distinction. Do not describe a successful defense and then
accidentally read the Strong attack damage against it.

Describe visible signs freely. If players expose the mechanism by lifting its
cover, show it. Ask for Reason when a search is uncertain and has a consequence,
not as a toll on every useful observation. Specialized abilities retain their
printed advantages; ordinary inspection does not acquire all their outcomes.

## Spring spear

*A polished scuff cuts across the dust before a narrow doorway. A dark slot in the
jamb is exactly the height of an unwary traveler's ribs.*

**Trigger and area:** weight on the marked threshold releases one spear into its
one-square space. A creature crossing without avoiding or disabling the plate is
the target. If several cross, only the first activation fires the loaded spear.

**Resolution:** Agility defense; no inherent Boons or Banes. Deal physical damage
according to the defense result.

| Weak defense | Standard defense | Strong defense |
|---|---|---|
| 5 physical | 3 physical | 1 physical |

**Timing:** one discharge. Someone must physically reload and reset the mechanism
before it can fire again; it does not reload itself each round.

**Counterplay:** the plate can be stepped over or avoided with a legal route once
noticed. An exposed catch can be wedged safely when there is time and access. If a
hero must reach it while under immediate pressure, use a Main Action and an Agility
roll: Weak holds the catch only while the hero stays and holds it; Standard wedges
it for passage; Strong also leaves it easy to release deliberately. Explain these
stakes before the attempt. A suitable specialist card may provide its own method.

**Aftermath:** the discharged spear and slot are obvious. No lingering Bleeding is
added unless an explicit effect applies it. Do not erase the party's work by
resetting the trap when they return.

## Furnace vent

*A red glow pulses beneath the grate. Its shutter hangs open, and the release
lever shudders with every breath of the bellows.*

**Trigger and area:** operating the bellows while the shutter is open releases a
burst into the three consecutive squares directly in front of the grate. Mark
those squares visibly. The bellows requires someone to operate it; this entry
adds no automatic hazard turn.

**Resolution:** each exposed player rolls Agility defense; no inherent modifiers.
The table shows complete outcomes.

| Weak defense | Standard defense | Strong defense |
|---|---|---|
| 3 Fire and Burning 2 | 2 Fire and Burning 1 | 1 Fire |

**Timing:** resolve a burst when the bellows is operated. In combat, operating it
requires a Main Action by someone who can reach its handle. Characters can see
and interrupt an operator through their ordinary abilities; operating machinery
is not automatically a new opportunity-attack trigger.

**Counterplay:** leave the marked squares, stop the operator, or close the adjacent
release lever with a Maneuver and no roll. A closed shutter prevents further
bursts until someone reopens it with a Maneuver. Clearly visible controls offer
an immediate useful choice.

**Aftermath:** existing Burning resolves and decays normally even after the shutter
closes. A plausible Main Action can reduce it by 1. Closing the vent does not itself
heal damage or remove Burning. Include the vent's possible pressure when evaluating
any combat fought here; its CV has not been established.

## Sluice gate

*The gate's chain jumps with every surge. Water carries leaves through the open
channel, tracing a straight path toward a stone catch basin.*

**Trigger and area:** opening the gate releases one surge along a marked line of
six channel squares, one square wide. It affects creatures in those squares at
that instant; this is not a persistent aura or an attack on everyone who enters later.

**Resolution:** Agility defense, no inherent modifiers. Each affected player rolls.

| Weak defense | Standard defense | Strong defense |
|---|---|---|
| Push 3 downstream | Push 2 downstream | Push 1 downstream |

**Timing:** one surge per filled reservoir. The reservoir takes an hour to refill;
there is no second surge during the same combat. The gate can be opened by an
adjacent Main Action without a roll.

**Counterplay:** stand on the raised walkway outside the channel, close the gate
before it releases, or interrupt the operator with an applicable ability. The gate
cannot be closed retroactively after the surge resolves. A ready escape route
should be visible before someone steps into the channel.

**Aftermath:** apply Stability and terrain costs normally. This entry deals no direct
damage, but collisions along its forced path use the ordinary collision rules.
The catch basin has a flat floor at the channel's level, not an unannounced lethal
drop. If you place a drop there, describe and evaluate that additional danger.

## The whispering inscription

*Words drift at the edge of the reader's attention. For a moment, every nearby voice
sounds like their own, speaking half a sentence too late.*

**Trigger and area:** a creature deliberately reads the exposed inscription within
one square. Merely seeing the stone from a distance does not activate it. Warn the
players with its audible echoes and the writer's nearby note, “Cover before copying.”

**Resolution:** Reason defense, no inherent modifiers.

| Weak defense | Standard defense | Strong defense |
|---|---|---|
| Dazed 2 | Dazed 1 | No condition |

**Timing:** once per reader during the scene. Looking away and back does not repeat
it. This scene limit belongs to this inscription, not to every magical hazard.

**Counterplay:** cover the writing without reading it, turn the tablet face down,
or use a suitable identification ability from a permitted position to learn its
properties. This is an object with a specific effect, not permission to invent a
generic counterspell roll. Reading it is optional; do not place the adventure's
only essential clue behind an unavoidable activation.

**Aftermath:** Dazed affects action-category selection at the start of the affected
turn and decays at its end. It does not undo spent actions. In a safe scene, allow
the temporary distraction to pass; do not inflict permanent mental scars merely
because its description is unsettling.

## A blocked escape

*Fresh rubble fills the service passage. Dust still falls from a timber that is
bending under the stones.*

This is an obstacle, not an automatic damaging trap. State whether the heroes have
time to clear it safely. With adequate time, tools and no danger, describe the work.
Under pressure, declare its progress target and what another attempt will cost.

**Example configuration:** outside combat, up to three heroes can work abreast.
Clearing a passage is cumulative, target 8, one minute per attempt. Each contributing
Brawn roll adds 1/2/3 progress. If pursuing guards will arrive in two minutes, announce
the warning before the first attempt. Weak, Standard and Strong add 6; a later
Weak and Weak add the remaining 2. The party must still decide who watches the route.

A supported alternate path or a card that changes the obstruction can provide a
different solution. During combat, use individual actions such as Force; no group
check is available. Do not add a collapsing-ceiling damage roll unless that danger
was separately established.

## Travel, water and heights

Use weather and terrain to create choices. A flooded ford can make the longer
bridge route attractive. A storm may make arrival before dark uncertain. Tell the
players the consequences of each known route; do not demand a travel roll simply
because another day passes.

Climbing and swimming normally cost two movement per square. This assumes the
surface or water is traversable. Specialist cards may improve what the hero can do.
Safe jumps within the ordinary Agility-based limits need no roll. Risky execution
can call for Agility after you establish what is at stake.

Falling uses the existing rate of 3 HP per two squares fallen. Do not conceal a
fall's height until after a hero jumps. Resolve exceptional surfaces and landing
circumstances openly; a hazard entry should state any specific additional condition
rather than assuming every fall applies Prone or a wound.

## Hazards during combat

Before initiative, identify a hazard's trigger, affected area, frequency and the
cost of operating or disabling it. If it repeats, write down exactly when. Do not
quietly give it a full creature turn, an opportunity attack or a new CV because
it sits on a battle map.

A creature that knows a trap can try to use its terrain and controls within its
normal actions. That knowledge does not automatically make it immune. Players who
learn the same mechanism should be able to exploit it under the same procedure.

After play, record which hazards created useful decisions and which merely added
bookkeeping. Remove repeated rolls that do not change the situation. A danger the
heroes recognize and cleverly avoid has done its job.
