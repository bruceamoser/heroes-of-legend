# Treasure and Rewards

A reward gives the heroes another choice. Coins can buy passage or supplies. A
recipe can turn future ingredients into something useful. An introduction can
open a door that no weapon would open safely. Choose rewards that make the next
adventure more interesting and acknowledge what the company accomplished.

Use the [ordinary market](../../market.md) as the common price reference. HOL uses
**coins**, with no metal denominations. Prices do not increase merely because a
hero gains a level.

## Begin with useful scale

A simple meal costs 1 coin and a plain bed costs 2 per night, meals separate. A
Rope Coil costs 5; an ordinary melee weapon costs 12; a Lesser Healing Bottle costs
20. These anchors help you describe what a reward means to people in the world.

There is no established mandatory treasure total per level in this manuscript.
Consider the party's current supplies, actual needs and opportunities to spend.
A reward can be generous without buying a new discipline rank: coins purchase
objects and services, while DP purchases character development.

| Reward | What the party gains | What it does not supply |
|---|---|---|
| Coins | Flexible purchasing power where goods are available | DP, discipline ranks or class signatures |
| Equipment | A specific physical object | Unprinted training or an extra attack |
| A supply | Its stated inventory die and uses | Guaranteed charges equal to the die's faces |
| A recipe | Knowledge of a particular product | Crafting capability, tools or ingredients by itself |
| A service | An agreed attempt or period of work | Automatic Strong outcomes or unrestricted daily treatment |
| Information or access | A useful fact, route, introduction or permission | Authority over everyone who hears of it |

## Put treasure where it belongs

Decide who owns an object and why it is there. A tool chest in a workshop, a road
toll in a guard's pouch, and a carefully wrapped recipe in a cook's kit all tell
something about their owners. The party can find useful goods without every
creature carrying a purse calibrated to its CV.

Creature loot entries offer starting material. Adjust an individual's possessions
to the scene before play: a bandit who has collected no tolls should not carry the
same takings as one leaving a successful robbery. Keep the equipment required for
its printed actions consistent with its description. Do not remove essential
gear solely because the party might recover it.

Give rewards for resolving the problem. A negotiation that opens a route may
fulfill the same contract as a fight. If rescuing a captive is the objective,
payment should not secretly depend on defeating the captors afterward.

> **Example: the crossing commission.** A patron promises 80 coins to the company
> for a rescue and safe access. Four heroes can divide that into 20 each. The rescued
> workers also offer a Rope Coil and Lever and Wedges, each worth 5. If accepted,
> those are two carried items, not 10 extra coins in addition to the items. The
> party earns the contract payment whether it negotiates with the robbers or fights.

## Selling and trading

Intact, useful gear sells for **half its listed price, rounded down**, or trades
at **full listed value** with a willing trader. Pay a shortfall in coins. Unused
trade value is not cash change, and a trade cannot be converted into coins or a
cash-equivalent voucher at full value.

> **Example: a recovered weapon.** An ordinary melee weapon is worth 12 coins.
> Selling it brings 6. A willing trader can instead accept it for 12 coins of goods:
> a 5-coin rope, a 4-coin sleeping roll and a 3-coin water flask. The party cannot
> trade it for a 12-coin credit note and immediately cash that note for 12 coins.

A market needs willing buyers as well as prices. Do not promise that a village
will purchase unlimited identical goods. Equally, avoid inventing shortages only
after a player has committed to a reasonable purchase. Establish unusual local
conditions in advance and quote the offered price clearly.

Supplies with uncertain remaining quantities have no standard resale offer.
A freshly acquired or crafted full supply can attract a half-price offer where
there is demand. Special equipment needs an available buyer and an agreed valuation;
apply the same half-sale/full-trade rule to that valuation. Gems and other treasure
with a stated trade value do not suffer a second automatic halving.

Selling a card-granted item does not renew the grant. Learning an ability that
uses a compatible item already owned does not create another copy to sell. The
same ordinary weapon can serve several compatible attacks, with its Bulk counted once.

## Supply is uncertain, not imaginary

After using an inventory supply, roll its die. On 1, step down:
**d20 → d12 → d10 → d8 → d6 → d4 → out**. The use that empties it still works.
The die tracks remaining supply without counting every arrow or mouthful.

A d4 healing bottle has a 25% chance to empty after each use. That does not mean
it contains exactly four doses. A supply that has survived several checks remains
at its current die until a check changes it.

The current market's healing bottles use d4: Lesser restores 6 HP, Greater 10,
and Superior 16, before Life resistance and missing-HP limits. Their price buys
potency as well as uncertain longevity. They do not require a healing spell roll
or caster HP payment.

A potion still needs a free hand and the giver's Maneuver. A different recipient
spends a Reaction unless at 0 HP. A bow, two-handed weapon, or weapon-and-shield
combination occupies the hands that would otherwise use the bottle. Dropping an
item is free; properly stowing it takes a Maneuver and can provoke. Apply those
costs consistently to heroes and opponents.

## Make special equipment personal

Ordinary weapons describe what the hero carries; their attack cards supply the
mechanics. Special equipment adds the benefit actually printed on its card.
A named sword does not acquire extra range or damage from its appearance alone.

An [Ember Edge](../../equipment/ember-edge.md) requires Flame 1 and adds 1 Fire
damage to attacks made with that melee weapon, with Burning 1 on Strong. It does
not turn a ranged attack into a compatible use. A hero may own it before meeting
the requirement, but cannot use its special benefit until qualified.

A [Cincture of Might](../../equipment/cincture-of-might.md) gives its printed Brawn
benefit while worn. It does not stack with another positive Brawn item and does
not recalculate creation DP or class eligibility. Use explicit card text for cursed
items that suppress bonuses or set an attribute; a curse is not permission to
invent a hidden exception after its bearer acts.

You can change an item's history and appearance to fit a campaign. A brass lens
may come from a lighthouse or a buried observatory without changing its ability.
Make mechanical changes explicit and review their effect on the party before
introducing them as routine treasure.

## Understanding what was found

An item states whether understanding is required to use it. Some magical weapons
work at once; another object may require identification. Use the appropriate
identification card and reveal what its outcome says. Possessing a mysterious
object does not grant every fact about it, but ordinary inspection still reveals
its visible form and obvious operation.

Give useful clues to dangerous properties. A written warning, an earlier owner's
account or an observed effect lets the party investigate before committing. A
curse can create a difficult decision without depending on the players having
no chance to recognize risk.

## Recipes, ingredients and work

Crafting requires an appropriate capability, a known recipe, its tools and actual
ingredients. Cards that grant recipes provide those stated choices; additional
recipes can come through Ordinary Recipe Study, discovery or derivation. They
are not automatically available for coin purchase.

An identified object is not automatically a known recipe. Derivation uses its own
ability, work intervals and cumulative target. Rare objects may require a long
research project. Do not shorten that project through group rolls; neither crafting
nor derivation uses group checks.

> **Example: Bottled Morning.** A crafter with this ability knows the Lesser Healing
> Bottle recipe. With a mixing kit, moonmint, clean water and prepared life salts,
> they spend one hour and roll Knowledge. The outcome raises one compatible supply
> by 1/2/3 inventory steps, but the recipe's cap is d4. From out, any result therefore
> produces a d4 bottle. Strong does not create three bottles or exceed the cap.
> Check carried ingredients after the attempt. The recipe is not free stock.

The ordinary d6 material bundle costs 12 coins, while prepared healing ingredients
cost 80 for their d6 bundle. Each bundle must contain the materials the recipe
actually needs. Renaming common grass as life salts does not satisfy it. Foraging
cards give practical ways to acquire suitable materials and deserve places where
those materials can be found.

## Services and lasting favors

A treatment appointment costs 10 coins for one eligible attempt; it does not
promise complete recovery. Identification service likewise buys an attempt with
the right capability, not a guaranteed Strong result. Daily limits still apply
across different providers.

An ally might waive a fee, offer a workshop, or introduce a specialist as a reward.
State the scope: one appointment, use of the bench for a week, or an introduction.
This makes favors useful without turning every grateful person into a permanent
source of unlimited labor. Hired people retain their own concerns and choices.
